The best Bible translation for kids depends on how well your child reads, and the honest short answer is a ladder. **Start at the NIrV or ICB (written at a third-grade level), move to the NLT around fourth to sixth grade, then to the NIV or CSB, and save the ESV, NASB and KJV for teens.** Below is the evidence for that ladder from the publishers themselves, checked on November 16, 2026, plus the same two verses in every translation so you can hear the difference.

One distinction first: this guide is about **translations**, the actual Bible text. A storybook Bible (like The Jesus Storybook Bible) retells selected stories and isn't a translation at all. For specific Bibles to buy at each age, see our [best kids Bibles by age](/blog/best-bibles-for-kids).

## The Translations, Easiest to Hardest

| Translation | Reading level | Approach | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NIrV** (New International Reader's Version) | Third grade, per Biblica | Simplified NIV, shorter sentences | Ages 6 to 10 |
| **ICB** (International Children's Bible) | Third grade, per Thomas Nelson | Translated specifically for children | Ages 6 to 10 |
| **CEV** (Contemporary English Version) | "Grade schoolers," per the American Bible Society | Plain-English translation | Ages 7 to 11 |
| **NLT** (New Living Translation) | About sixth grade (BibleGateway, Christianbook) | Meaning-based | Ages 9 to 13 |
| **GNT** (Good News Translation) | About seventh grade (BibleGateway) | Thought-for-thought | Ages 10 and up |
| **NIV** (New International Version) | Seventh to eighth grade, per Biblica | Balanced | Ages 11 and up |
| **CSB** (Christian Standard Bible) | Seventh grade average, per its publisher | "Optimal equivalence" | Ages 11 and up |
| **NKJV** (New King James Version) | Eighth grade, per Thomas Nelson | Word for word | Teens |
| **ESV** (English Standard Version) | About tenth grade (BibleGateway) | Essentially literal | Teens |
| **NASB** (New American Standard Bible) | About eleventh grade (BibleGateway) | Word for word | Older teens |
| **KJV** (King James Version) | About twelfth grade (BibleGateway) | Formal, 1611 English | Older teens and adults |

A caution about those grade levels: BibleGateway and Christianbook both say their figures come mostly from the publishers and that not everyone agrees on the formulas. No translation we could find has a published Lexile measure. Treat the numbers as a guide to the order, not a precise test.

## Hear the Difference: Psalm 23:1

The clearest way to choose is to read one verse aloud in each. Here's Psalm 23:1, quoted from BibleGateway:

- **NIrV:** "The Lord is my shepherd. He gives me everything I need."
- **ICB:** "The Lord is my shepherd. I have everything I need."
- **CEV:** "You, Lord, are my shepherd. I will never be in need."
- **NLT:** "The Lord is my shepherd; I have all that I need."
- **NIV:** "The Lord is my shepherd, I lack nothing."
- **CSB:** "The Lord is my shepherd; I have what I need."
- **ESV, NKJV and KJV:** "The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want."

A six-year-old understands "I have everything I need" on the first read. "I shall not want" is beautiful, and most young children hear it as "I don't want the Lord," which is exactly backwards. That one verse is the whole case for starting simple.

## And John 3:16

- **NIrV:** "God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son. Anyone who believes in him will not die but will have eternal life."
- **ICB:** "For God loved the world so much that he gave his only Son. God gave his Son so that whoever believes in him may not be lost, but have eternal life."
- **NLT:** "For this is how God loved the world: He gave his one and only Son, so that everyone who believes in him will not perish but have eternal life."
- **NIV:** "For God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son, that whoever believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life."
- **KJV:** "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life."

Notice the NIrV and ICB split one long sentence into two, and swap "perish" for "not die" or "not be lost." That's what a children's translation does: the same meaning, in sentences a young reader can hold.

## The Main Options, Explained

**NIrV.** Biblica describes it as using "the text of the NIV" adapted for "a third-grade reading level," built for "children ages 6 to 10" and as a "stepping-stone to the NIV." Last revised in 2014 to match the 2011 NIV. If your church reads the NIV, this is the natural first Bible, because the memory verses will sound almost the same when they graduate.

**ICB.** Thomas Nelson calls it "the first translation of the Holy Scriptures prepared specifically for children," written at a third-grade level, and stresses it is "not a storybook or a paraphrased Bible." It cites a Vanderbilt study by Dr. Charles K. Kinzer that found it the easiest version for children to comprehend. A good choice if your church uses the NKJV or isn't tied to one translation.

**CEV.** The American Bible Society says it is "easily read by grade schoolers" and "not a paraphrase." Clear and warm, though fewer kids' Bibles are built on it.

**NLT.** Tyndale says the translators kept "both formal-equivalence and dynamic-equivalence in mind": literal where that reads naturally, more meaning-based where a literal rendering would confuse. It's the translation behind the NLT Hands-On Bible, and often the one kids keep into their teens.

**NIV and CSB.** The middle of the ladder. Biblica places the NIV at a seventh-to-eighth-grade level (its own pages say both), and the CSB's publisher gives a seventh-grade average. Both are what many churches read from the pulpit, which makes them a good target for ages 11 and up.

**ESV, NKJV, NASB, KJV.** The word-for-word translations, prized for study and memorization and harder for young readers. One note for ESV families: Crossway released an **ESV Text Edition: 2025**, changing 42 verses, so printings during 2025 and 2026 may differ slightly. And **The Message** is a paraphrase, not a translation; it's a lovely companion for adults, not a child's main Bible.

## Which Kids' Bibles Use Which Translation

- **Adventure Bible** (Zondervan): NIV, plus an **NIrV** edition for early readers, and NKJV and NASB editions.
- **Hands-On Bible** (Tyndale): NLT.
- **ICB Holy Bible** (Thomas Nelson): ICB.
- **CSB Kids Bible** and **Big Picture Interactive Bible** (B&H): CSB.
- **ESV Children's Bible**, **Seek and Find** and **Big Picture** editions (Crossway): ESV.

## How to Choose, in Three Questions

1. **Can they read it out loud without stumbling?** Open to Psalm 23 and have them read it. If they stumble every line, go one step easier.
2. **What does your church read?** Matching the family of translation (NIrV leading to NIV, for instance) means the verses they memorize match what they hear on Sunday.
3. **Will they read it alone, or with you?** A translation a step harder works fine read together; for independent reading, pick the level they can handle on their own.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best Bible translation for kids?

For ages 6 to 10, the NIrV or ICB, both written at a third-grade reading level according to their publishers. Around ages 9 to 13, the NLT. From about 11 up, the NIV or CSB. Save the ESV, NKJV, NASB and KJV for teens.

### What is the NIrV Bible?

The New International Reader's Version, published by Biblica. It uses the NIV's text simplified to a third-grade reading level with shorter sentences, made for children ages 6 to 10 and as a stepping-stone to the NIV. Its current edition was revised in 2014.

### Is the NIrV or ICB better for kids?

Both are written at a third-grade level, so either works. Choose the NIrV if your church reads the NIV, since the wording will carry over; choose the ICB if you want a translation prepared from the start specifically for children, or your family reads the NKJV.

### What is the easiest Bible translation to read?

Among full translations, the NIrV and ICB (third grade), then the CEV. The Message reads easily too, but it's a paraphrase, not a translation.

### At what age should a child read the NIV or ESV?

Biblica places the NIV at a seventh-to-eighth-grade level, so around ages 11 to 13. The ESV sits nearer tenth grade, so most kids are ready around 14 to 15, earlier if they read it alongside you.
