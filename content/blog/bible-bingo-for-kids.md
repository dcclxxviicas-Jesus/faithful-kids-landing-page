Bible bingo is a 5x5 grid game where each square holds a Bible character, story, or object instead of a number -- a caller reads clues, kids mark their squares, and the first to complete a row shouts "Bingo!" You can make a full set of cards in about 15 minutes with paper, a marker, and the four word lists in this guide.

It works for ages 4 to 12, scales from two kids at the kitchen table to thirty in a Sunday school room, and sneaks in real Bible review while everyone thinks they're just playing a game. Here's everything you need: how to make the cards, four complete themed word lists, four ways to play, and when each version works best.

## How Do You Make Bible Bingo Cards?

Draw a 5x5 grid, write "FREE" in the center square, and fill the other 24 squares with words from a master list of 25-30 -- shuffling the placement on every card so no two cards match. That's the whole craft. Step by step:

1. **Pick a theme and a master list.** Use one of the four lists below (characters, stories, objects, or Christmas). You need at least 25 words; 30 is better because it guarantees more variation between cards.
2. **Make the grids.** Draw a 5x5 grid on paper or print a blank bingo template. One card per player, plus a couple of spares.
3. **Fill the squares in a different order on each card.** The easy low-tech method: write the 30 words on a whiteboard, then fill each card by picking words in a scattered, random-feeling order. For young pre-readers, use simple stick-figure drawings or clip-art pictures instead of words.
4. **Make calling cards.** Write each master-list word on its own slip of paper or index card, and drop them all in a bowl. This is what the caller draws from.
5. **Grab markers.** Dry cereal, buttons, pennies, or torn paper squares all work. If you laminate the cards or use page protectors, dry-erase markers let you reuse the set for years.

That's it. If your kids enjoy making things, card creation itself is a great rainy-day activity -- hand each child a blank grid and the word list and let them make their own card, which doubles the review time. ### How to Print and Store the Sets

- **Card stock, not paper.** Bingo cards get handled by every kid in the room; paper cards last one session.
- **Page protectors plus dry-erase markers** turn any set into a reusable one and eliminate loose markers entirely.
- **Number the backs of the cards** (1 through 12) so you can quickly check which layouts are in play and re-collect the full set.
- **One large envelope per set,** labeled, holding the cards, the calling slips, and a printed copy of the word list. Sets that live in an envelope survive; sets in a pile do not.

## What Are the Best Bible Bingo Game Variations?

The four variations below cover everything from a 5-minute filler to a 40-minute event, and each changes the skill the game exercises.

### 1. Classic Line Bingo

The caller draws slips and reads the word (or a clue); players mark matching squares; first to complete five in a row -- across, down, or diagonal -- wins. Best for first-timers and mixed ages. Keep rounds short and play multiple rounds so more kids get a win. Pro tip: have the winner read their row back so you can verify against the called slips -- and so the group hears the words one more time.

### 2. Blackout (Cover-All)

Play continues until someone covers every square on their card. This is the long-game version -- 15 to 25 minutes -- and it's the right choice when you want the game to fill a whole session or keep kids occupied on a rainy afternoon. Because nearly every word gets called, every kid hears the full review list. Variation within the variation: "Four Corners" (cover the four corner squares) and "Picture Frame" (cover the entire outer edge) are good middle-length options.

### 3. Story-Clue Bingo

The caller never says the word on the card -- only a clue, a verse, or a one-sentence story summary, and players must figure out which square it points to. This is the most educational version and the best fit for ages 8+. Ramp difficulty by clue: easy ("He built the ark") to hard ("This judge defeated an army with 300 men, torches, and trumpets" -- Gideon, Judges 7). If your kids love the guessing element, they'll also enjoy our [Bible riddles for kids](/blog/bible-riddles-for-kids).

### 4. Action Bingo

When a word is called, kids must do a quick action before marking the square -- act out rowing for "Boat," flex muscles for "Samson," march in a circle for "Walls of Jericho." Best for ages 4-7 and for burning energy in a group setting. It's chaotic in the best way, and wiggly kids who can't sit through classic bingo will happily play three rounds of this.

## When Should You Use Bible Bingo?

Bible bingo shines in four situations: reviewing lessons in Sunday school, family game nights, holiday gatherings, and travel. A quick guide:

| Setting | Best Version | Why It Works |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| Sunday school review | Story-Clue Bingo | Reviews the quarter's lessons; clues test real knowledge |
| Family game night | Classic, best of 3 | Fast rounds, everyone can win one |
| Holiday gatherings | Blackout | Occupies a big mixed-age crowd for 20+ minutes |
| Road trips | Classic (laminated cards) | Reusable with dry-erase markers, no loose pieces needed if you use markers with clips |
| Ages 4-7 energy burn | Action Bingo | Movement keeps little ones engaged |

A few honest notes from experience. Bingo is a review tool, not a teaching tool -- kids get the most out of it when they already know the characters and stories on the card, so play it after a lesson or a season of lessons, not before. If your kids are learning the stories for the first time, pair the game with the source material: our video lessons at [Faithful Kids](https://faithfulkids.app/quiz) teach each Bible story with a quiz at the end, and a bingo card full of stories your kids have actually watched becomes ten times more fun.

Also, mind the competitiveness. With kids under 6, consider playing until everyone gets a bingo ("play to the last winner"), or hand out small stickers to every finisher. The goal is Bible words becoming familiar friends, not tears over a cereal-piece prize.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What ages is Bible bingo good for?

Bible bingo works from about age 4 through 12. Ages 4-6 do best with picture cards and Action Bingo; ages 7-9 handle word cards and classic rules; ages 10-12 stay engaged with Story-Clue Bingo, where the caller gives hints instead of naming the word. It's one of the few games where a 4-year-old and an 11-year-old can genuinely play together.

### How many words do I need for a Bible bingo game?

You need a master list of at least 25 words for a 5x5 card with a free center space, and 30 gives you better card variety. Each card uses 24 of the words in a shuffled arrangement. For younger kids, you can shrink to a 3x3 grid, which needs only a 12-15 word master list and produces much faster rounds.

### Can I play Bible bingo with a large Sunday school class?

Yes -- bingo scales beautifully to 20-30 kids, which is exactly why it's a Sunday school staple. Make 10-12 unique card layouts and it's fine if a few kids share the same layout, since duplicate cards just mean occasional simultaneous winners. Use Blackout or Picture Frame rules to stretch one round across a longer time slot.

### What can I use for bingo markers?

Dry cereal (like O-shaped cereal), buttons, pennies, dried beans, or small torn squares of construction paper all work well. If you laminate your cards or slide them into plastic page protectors, kids can mark squares with dry-erase markers instead -- no loose pieces, and the set becomes reusable for years.

### How do I make Bible bingo harder for older kids?

Switch to Story-Clue calling, where you read a clue, a paraphrased verse, or a story detail instead of the word itself -- players must identify the answer before they can mark it. You can also require winners to say one fact about each item in their winning row. Both changes turn the game into genuine trivia while keeping the bingo format kids love.

### Do I need to buy a Bible bingo set, or can I make my own?

You can absolutely make your own -- paper grids, a marker, and the word lists in this article are all it takes, and homemade cards let you match the words to exactly what your kids are learning. Purchased sets are more durable and save prep time, but they can't be customized to this month's lessons the way a homemade set can.
