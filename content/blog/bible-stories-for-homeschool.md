The simplest way to teach Bible stories in your homeschool is to walk through Scripture in order -- Genesis to Revelation -- at a pace of one story per school day, which covers the entire Bible in one 40-week school year. You do not need a seminary degree or an expensive boxed curriculum; you need a story order, a 15-minute daily rhythm, and a couple of activities that help each story stick.

This guide gives you all three: a complete 40-week scope and sequence, a daily lesson plan you can run tomorrow morning, and ways to adapt everything for a kindergartner, a fifth grader, or both at the same table.

## Why Teach the Bible Chronologically in Homeschool?

Teaching Bible stories in order gives kids something most Sunday school programs never do: the big picture. Most children know David and Goliath, Noah, and the manger scene as disconnected islands -- they have no idea David comes after the Exodus, or that the whole Old Testament is pointing toward Jesus. When you teach chronologically, your child watches one continuous story unfold: creation, fall, promise, rescue, kingdom, exile, Messiah, church.

That narrative arc is also what makes Bible time feel like "real school" instead of a devotional add-on. There is a scope, a sequence, and visible progress -- the same things that make a math curriculum work. ## How Do You Structure a Full Year of Bible Stories?

Here is a 40-week plan that covers the entire Bible in 20 units of two weeks each. Each unit has 10 stories -- one per school day, five days a week.

| Weeks | Unit | Book of the Bible |
|-------|--------|-------------------|
| 1-2 | Genesis | Genesis |
| 3-4 | Exodus | Exodus |
| 5-6 | Promised Land | Numbers/Joshua |
| 7-8 | Judges & Ruth | Judges/Ruth |
| 9-10 | Rise of Kings | 1 Samuel |
| 11-12 | King David's Reign | 2 Samuel/Psalms |
| 13-14 | Solomon & the Kingdom | 1 Kings |
| 15-16 | Elijah & Elisha | 1 & 2 Kings |
| 17-18 | Exile & Faith | Daniel/Jonah/Isaiah |
| 19-20 | The Return Home | Ezra/Nehemiah/Esther |
| 21-22 | Birth of Jesus | Matthew/Luke |
| 23-24 | Jesus Begins Ministry | Gospels |
| 25-26 | Teachings of Jesus | Matthew/Luke |
| 27-28 | Miracles of Jesus | Gospels |
| 29-30 | Road to the Cross | Gospels |
| 31-32 | He Is Risen | Gospels/Acts |
| 33-34 | Early Church | Acts |
| 35-36 | Paul's Adventures | Acts |
| 37-38 | Letters to Churches | Romans/Corinthians/Ephesians |
| 39-40 | Ending & Beginning | Hebrews/James/Revelation |

A few things to notice about this structure. The Old Testament gets 20 weeks and the New Testament gets 20 weeks, which keeps the year balanced -- many families accidentally spend eight months in the Old Testament and sprint through the Gospels in May. The two-week units also give you natural checkpoints: finish a unit, do a review day, celebrate, move on.

If you school four days a week instead of five, simply stretch each unit to two and a half weeks; the whole plan finishes in about 50 weeks, which still fits a calendar year with breaks.

## What Does a Daily Bible Lesson Look Like?

A homeschool Bible lesson does not need to be long -- it needs to be daily. Fifteen to twenty minutes is the sweet spot for elementary ages. Here is a four-part rhythm that works:

1. **Story (3-5 minutes).** Read the passage from a storybook Bible or a real translation, or watch a short video version of the day's story. For younger kids, tell it in your own words with the Bible open on your lap.
2. **Check (2-3 minutes).** Ask three or four comprehension questions: Who was in the story? What happened? What surprised you? A quick oral quiz tells you instantly whether the story landed or bounced.
3. **Discuss (5 minutes).** Ask one heart-level question -- "When have you felt like Joseph in the pit?" -- and let silence do its work. Our bank of [Bible discussion questions for kids](/blog/bible-discussion-questions-for-kids) has ready-made questions for dozens of stories.
4. **Activity (5-10 minutes).** Copywork, a journal entry, a drawing, a coloring page, or a map exercise. Rotate these so no single one wears out.

The order matters: story first, questions second, activity last. Kids engage with the discussion far better when the story is seconds old, not minutes.

## How Do You Adapt Bible Lessons by Age?

**Ages 4-6 (pre-K and kindergarten):** Keep it to ten minutes. Focus on what happened, not what it means -- narrative before theology. Let them narrate the story back to you in their own words, then color. Our library of [Bible coloring pages for kids](/blog/bible-coloring-pages-for-kids) pairs well with almost every story in the 40-week plan.

**Ages 7-9:** This is the golden window for the full routine above. Add a weekly memory verse -- one verse per story unit, reviewed daily, is realistic. The techniques in our guide to [Bible memory verse strategies for kids](/blog/bible-memory-verse-strategies-for-kids) will save you a lot of repetition frustration.

**Ages 10-12:** Move from storybook Bibles to a readable real translation (NIrV or NLT). Add written narration: three to five sentences summarizing the story, plus one sentence answering "What does this show about God?" By fifth or sixth grade, kids can also start locating each story on a timeline and a map.

**Teaching multiple ages together:** Do the story and discussion as a group, then split for activities -- the six-year-old colors while the ten-year-old writes. One story, two outputs. This is the single biggest sanity-saver for homeschool moms teaching a spread of ages.

## What Activities Help Bible Stories Stick?

Rotate through these five, roughly one per weekday:

- **Copywork:** The child copies the key verse of the story in their best handwriting. Doubles as penmanship.
- **Narration journal:** They retell the story in writing (or dictate it to you for pre-writers). Over 40 weeks this becomes a keepsake -- the whole Bible in your child's own words.
- **Timeline notebook:** Add each story to a growing paper timeline taped to the schoolroom wall. By spring, kids can literally see that Moses comes before David and David before Daniel.
- **Map work:** Trace the Exodus route, Paul's journeys, and Israel's borders. Geography makes the stories feel like history instead of fairy tales.
- **Printables and games:** Word searches, mazes, and matching pages give lighter days a win. Our roundup of [free Bible printables for kids](/blog/free-bible-printables-for-kids) covers most major stories at no cost.

## How Do You Track Progress Without Turning Bible Into a Graded Subject?

Most homeschool families do not want to slap a letter grade on Scripture, and you do not need to. Track three simple things instead: stories completed (check off the 200), verses memorized (aim for 20 over the year -- one per unit), and narrations written. That gives you a portfolio for your homeschool records in states that require them, without ever making the Bible feel like a test to pass.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can Bible stories count as a homeschool subject?
Yes. In most states, Bible can be logged as its own subject or folded into reading, history, or character education. Copywork counts toward handwriting, narration toward composition, and map work toward geography. Check your state's requirements, but few states restrict what you may teach -- they only specify what you must.

### How many days per week should I teach the Bible in homeschool?
Five short lessons beat one long one. At one story per day, five days a week, you finish the whole Bible in 40 weeks. If your schedule only allows three or four days, keep the same order and let the plan take a little longer -- consistency matters more than pace.

### What Bible translation is best for homeschool lessons?
For ages 4-7, a storybook Bible is fine. From about age 8, use an easy full translation such as the NIrV (3rd grade reading level) or NLT. Reading from a real Bible -- even with your help -- teaches kids that the stories come from Scripture itself, not just from a picture book.

### Should I teach the Old and New Testament in the same year?
Yes, if you can. Splitting them across two years leaves young kids with a foggy memory of Genesis by the time they reach the Gospels. The 40-week plan above gives each testament 20 weeks, so the connections between promise and fulfillment stay fresh.

### What if my child gets bored during Bible time?
Shorten the lesson before you abandon it -- ten focused minutes beat twenty distracted ones. Then vary the input: alternate reading aloud, video versions, and letting your child read to you. Boredom usually signals that the format has gone stale, not that the child has rejected the Bible.

### How do I teach hard stories like Noah's flood or the crucifixion?
Tell the truth at the child's altitude. For young kids, keep the focus on God's rescue and promise rather than dwelling on the destruction. For older kids, let the hard parts raise honest questions -- "Why did God do that?" is not a crisis, it is the curriculum working.
