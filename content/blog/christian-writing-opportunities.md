If you're a Christian parent, teacher, pastor, or homeschool educator with an essay in you, the bedtime conversation that changed how you pray, the Sunday school lesson that went sideways and taught you more than seminary did, there are real outlets that want it. Fewer than there used to be, and the lists floating around the internet are badly out of date, so for this guide we checked every outlet's own submission page in November 2026 and only included the ones that are verifiably open.

One thing upfront, because this guide follows our house rule of saying where we stand: Faithful Kids runs a parenting section, [Raising Faithful Kids](/parenting), that accepts essay and op-ed pitches, so it's on this list, clearly labeled, with the same honest treatment as everyone else. Several outlets here are better choices than us depending on what you write. We'd rather you land the right home for your piece than pitch us the wrong one.

## Risen Motherhood

**What they want:** Gospel-centered articles for moms. Their guidelines ask for roughly 30% personal narrative and 70% gospel application, your story is the doorway, Scripture is the room. They want a specific lesson the reader can apply, and they encourage vulnerable, vivid writing.

**Length and format:** Their guidelines put articles at 800–1,000 words, written in their style and tone, using the ESV or CSB for Bible references.

**How to submit:** Email the full finished article as a Word attachment to submissions@risenmotherhood.com, with a three-to-five-sentence description of the piece in the email body. If you haven't heard back in four to six weeks, they say to assume a pass and submit elsewhere, which is kinder than most outlets, who simply never tell you.

**Honest note:** This is one of the most recognized names in Christian motherhood writing, which means competition is real. A piece that's 70% your story and 30% application is the most common reason for a pass, they want the ratio the other way around.

## Christian Parenting (christianparenting.org)

**What they want:** Parenting articles that, in their words, rely heavily on Scripture and Jesus, practical pieces that point readers to God's Word. Suggest one or two titles and include three to five subtitles; their editors may retitle and restructure.

**Format:** They're unusually specific: a Word document, one-inch margins, 12-point Times New Roman, single space after periods, lowercase pronouns for God, Bible citations in parentheses with the translation named on first use, and a bio of 150 words or fewer with a headshot.

**How to submit:** Through the contributor form on their site.

**Honest note:** Contributors agree to share the published article on their own social media and tag the site. If you have no social presence at all, factor that in. The formatting rules read as fussy, but following them exactly is the cheapest way to signal you're easy to work with, editors notice.

## Just Between Us

**What they want:** Personal, conversational articles that encourage and equip Christian women, relatable stories with practical, Scripture-based application. They publish to weekly topics (their guidelines list upcoming themes and deadlines), and they also take off-topic pieces by email. No poetry, no fiction, no book reviews, and, stated outright, no AI-generated articles.

**Length and pay:** 800–1,000 words. Unpaid; contributors receive a one-month digital subscription. They ask for first rights.

**How to submit:** Email a Word document to submissions@justbetweenus.org with a title page (title, name, email, word count) and a two-to-four-sentence bio. NIV unless you note otherwise; capitalize pronouns for God, the opposite of Christian Parenting's rule, which is a good reminder to read each outlet's style sheet rather than reusing one manuscript everywhere.

**Honest note:** If you haven't heard back in six to eight weeks, you're free to submit the piece elsewhere. Their special issues (grandparent grief, digital boundaries for parents, adult children going no contact) have no deadline and are the least crowded door in.

## Reformed Perspective

**What they want:** Reformed reflection on cultural trends and current events, technology, economics, education, family life, written for the whole family and answering two questions: why should Christians care, and what might they do? The audience is conservatively Reformed, so write inside that tradition or don't pitch here.

**Length and pay:** Features run 600–2,000 words (they say they may not read anything over 2,500), and accepted original articles earn an honorarium of $100–$300, one of the few outlets on this list with stated pay for essays.

**How to submit:** Via the Submit an Article link on their site, with your name, church affiliation, and relevant experience. Original material only, and use a modern translation, their guidelines specifically say no KJV.

## Focus on the Family Clubhouse

This one is different: you're not writing *about* parenting, you're writing *for* kids, Clubhouse is Focus on the Family's magazine for ages 8–12. It belongs on this list because it's a genuinely paying market open to unknown writers, and the parents, teachers, and Sunday school leaders reading this are exactly the people who know how kids actually talk.

**What they want:** Fiction of 1,800–2,000 words (historical, contemporary, even fantasy, with kids as the main characters solving their own problems) and nonfiction of 400–1,000 words: real kids doing extraordinary things, activities, humor, interviews. No preachy stories with heavy morals, no Bible retellings, no poetry.

**Pay:** 15–25 cents per word, paid on acceptance, with a contract. That's $270–$500 for a full-length story.

**How to submit:** Completed manuscripts only, no query letters, by postal mail (yes, really: typed, double-spaced, with a self-addressed stamped envelope for the reply). No reprints, no simultaneous submissions, and seasonal material needs to arrive at least eight months early. They aim to respond within twelve weeks.

## Plough

**What they want:** Plough publishes nonfiction articles, reviews, art, and letters for its website and quarterly print magazine. Their essays are narrative-driven, built around a real-life story rather than an argued thesis, though they'll consider an idea-driven piece if the subject is urgent. Faith, family, community, and society are home turf. They do not take unsolicited fiction or poetry, those go through their annual contests (covered, with the rest of the field, in our [Christian writing contests guide](/blog/christian-writing-contests)).

**How to submit:** Pitches or finished manuscripts to submissions@plough.com with a one-paragraph bio or CV and links to previous work if you have any. Their own advice is the best advice: read the magazine first. Plough has a distinct literary sensibility, and a piece that would be perfect for Risen Motherhood will feel out of place there, and vice versa.

## Guideposts

**What they want:** True, first-person stories about everyday people changed by an experience, told in scenes, focused on one specific event, showing how faith helped resolve a real challenge. You can write someone else's story with their permission. What they explicitly do *not* want, in their own words: essays, sermons, testimonials, fiction, or poetry, which makes Guideposts the mirror image of most outlets on this list. If your piece argues a point, it belongs elsewhere; if something *happened* to you, it belongs here.

**Length and pay:** Full-length stories run 1,200–1,500 words, shorter department pieces 300–600. Guideposts pays, with payment made when the story is accepted for publication (rates aren't posted).

**How to submit:** Email submissions@guideposts.org (their *Angels on Earth* magazine takes angelic-encounter stories separately at submissions@angelsonearth.org).

## For Catholic Writers

The Catholic outlets run their own ecosystem with its own expectations, and two have clear, current, verifiable guidelines.

### CatholicMom.com

**What they want:** Regular columnists rather than one-off guest posts; they ask writers to be practicing Catholics and to contribute at least four times a year, roughly every other month. Writers are volunteers, keep copyright to their work, and are expected to share their own and other contributors' pieces.

**How to apply:** A contributor survey plus three nonfiction writing samples as Word documents, ideally up to 1,200 words each, a bio of 65 words or fewer, and a headshot. Questions go to editor@catholicmom.com.

**Notable rule:** Their AI policy (dated April 2026) excludes content written partly or fully by AI, even if you edit it afterward; though AI for research, brainstorming, or grammar tools is allowed.

### Catholic Exchange

**What they want:** Reflections on the spiritual and practical life of the Catholic faith, prayer, the sacraments, the saints, at 800–1,200 words. Unpaid; they say plainly they can't offer financial compensation.

**How to submit:** New authors use the form on their submissions page with a one-paragraph bio and a headshot of at least 500×500 pixels; returning authors email submissions@catholicexchange.com. If you hear nothing in two weeks, consider it declined.

**Notable rules:** Articles must be fully written by the human author; they run submissions through an AI detector and factor the result into the decision. On publication they keep full rights, including reprints, and edited versions aren't sent back for approval; know that going in.

## For Kids' Ministry Leaders

If you teach Sunday school or lead a children's ministry, two established sites take practitioner contributions.

### Ministry-to-Children

**What they want:** Original Bible lesson plans, craft ideas with directions, ministry stories, practical tips, and curriculum reviews, the working material of kids' ministry. Two firm rules: nothing copyrighted (including a purchased lesson plan you've tweaked), and nothing already published elsewhere online.

**How to submit:** Email Tony@ministry-to-children.com. Pay isn't stated; the site's model is free content for ministry workers.

### ChurchLeaders

**What they want:** Articles, feature stories, how-tos, and resources from anyone serving in a church; their page says the site is for "every aspiring leader, volunteer, or staff person," not just pastors. Five contribution routes are listed on their contribute page, from suggesting an article to becoming a news scout.

**Notable rule:** Their open policy means what you contribute freely is shared freely; this is visibility and service, not a paying market.

## A Caution About Older Lists

Round-up lists in this niche rot fast. As of November 2026 we could not find live, current submission guidelines for several outlets that still appear on them, Her View From Home, Grow Christians, Rooted Ministry, and MOPS (whose most recent public calls for submissions date back several years), and we couldn't reach Word on Fire's submissions page to verify it. The Gospel Coalition's regional editions post their own guidelines, [Australia's](https://au.thegospelcoalition.org/submit-an-article-to-tgca) accepts submissions primarily from residents of Australia and New Zealand (unpaid, 1,000 words maximum, with a strict generative-AI declaration), but we couldn't locate current public guidelines for the US site. None of this means those outlets are closed; it means you should read the live page yourself before writing a word for any of them.

## How to Actually Get Accepted

Having read all twelve outlets' guidelines side by side, the pattern is unmistakable:

1. **Follow the formatting rules exactly.** Three of these outlets dedicate more words to formatting than to content. That's not pedantry; it's a filter for writers who are easy to edit.
2. **Match the ratio.** Nearly every faith outlet wants your story as the vehicle, not the destination. Risen Motherhood says it in numbers: 30% narrative, 70% application. If your draft is all anecdote, it's not done.
3. **Write it yourself.** Just Between Us bans AI-generated articles outright; we don't publish AI-written personal accounts either, and editors everywhere are getting good at spotting them. A personal essay a machine wrote is a contradiction in terms; the life in it is the only thing being bought.
4. **One manuscript per outlet.** NIV here, ESV there; capitalize pronouns here, lowercase them there. Reusing a single file everywhere signals you read none of the guidelines.
5. **Respect the silence windows.** Four to six weeks at Risen Motherhood, six to eight at Just Between Us, twelve at Clubhouse. Then move on without guilt; simultaneous submissions are banned at some of these, so the clock matters.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do Christian websites pay for guest posts?

Mostly no; unpaid contribution with a byline is the norm for blogs and digital magazines in this niche. The paying exceptions on this list are Reformed Perspective ($100–$300 honorarium for features) and Focus on the Family Clubhouse (15–25 cents per word, writing for children). Treat any site that asks *you* to pay for placement as a hard no; that's a link-selling scheme, not a publication.

### How long should a Christian personal essay be?

The cluster is tight: 800–1,000 words at Risen Motherhood and Just Between Us, 600–2,000 at Reformed Perspective. Draft at 1,200 and cut; an 800-word essay that lands is far easier to place than a 2,500-word one that wanders.

### Can I submit the same essay to several outlets at once?

Check each outlet's rules; Clubhouse explicitly refuses simultaneous submissions, while others simply ask for first rights. The safe pattern in this niche: one outlet at a time, and move on when its stated response window closes.

### Are there Catholic sites that accept submissions?

Yes; CatholicMom.com recruits volunteer columnists (practicing Catholics, roughly a column every other month), and Catholic Exchange takes one-off reflections of 800–1,200 words through a form, unpaid. Both have strict, explicit policies against AI-written content, and Catholic Exchange runs an AI detector on submissions. Details for both are above.

### What if I make videos rather than essays?

Different market, and it pays better: faith-based brands commission short video content from ordinary Christian families. Our guide to [Christian UGC](/blog/christian-ugc) covers how that works, and Faithful Kids [hires family creators directly](/creators).
