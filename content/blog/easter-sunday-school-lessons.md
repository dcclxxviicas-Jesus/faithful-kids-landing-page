Here are 10 free Easter Sunday school lessons that walk kids through Holy Week in order -- Palm Sunday, the temple, the Last Supper, the garden, the cross, the silent Saturday, and the empty tomb. Each lesson gives you an objective, the Scripture passage, a story script, discussion questions, one activity, and a memory verse, so a whole Easter unit takes about 15 minutes of prep per week.

Holy Week is the hardest week of the year to teach well. It contains the darkest event in the Bible and the brightest one, four days apart, and kids need both. The plans below tell the truth about the cross without dwelling on physical detail, and they keep the emotional weight where it belongs: on the empty tomb.

The rhythm for every lesson: **Story (10 min) → Discussion (10 min) → Activity (15 min) → Memory verse and prayer (5 min).** Every lesson also carries an **Age note** telling you exactly what to change for preschoolers versus elementary kids. If you'd rather have the whole week as a read-aloud narrative first, our [Holy Week day by day for kids](/blog/holy-week-day-by-day-for-kids) walks through the same sequence in story form.

## How Do You Teach the Easter Story Without Scaring Young Kids?

Tell the truth plainly, keep it short, and spend more time on Sunday than on Friday. Kids can hold "Jesus died to take the punishment for our sins, and on the third day God raised Him back to life" without a single graphic detail -- what frightens them is not the fact of the cross but lingering on the suffering, or leaving the story unresolved at the end of class. Never end a lesson on Friday. Even in the Good Friday lesson below, the last thing kids hear is that Sunday is coming.

## Lesson 1: Palm Sunday -- The King Arrives

**Objective:** Kids will explain why the crowds cheered for Jesus and what kind of king He turned out to be.

**Scripture:** Luke 19:28-40 (see also Matthew 21:1-11).

**Story:** Jesus rides into Jerusalem on a borrowed donkey while crowds throw cloaks and palm branches on the road and shout, "Blessed is the king who comes in the name of the Lord!" (Luke 19:38). Kings rode war horses. Jesus chose a donkey -- the animal of peace.

**Discussion questions:**
- What do people do today when someone famous arrives? What did the crowd do here?
- Why a donkey instead of a warhorse or a chariot?
- The crowd wanted a king who would beat the Romans. What kind of king did Jesus come to be?

**Activity:** Palm parade. Kids cut palm branches from green paper, then march a circuit of the room laying them down and shouting "Hosanna!" Explain that hosanna started as a cry for rescue: "Save us!"

**Memory verse:** "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord." -- Psalm 118:26

**Age note:** Preschoolers just do the parade -- marching, waving, and shouting one word is the whole lesson. Elementary kids can compare the two crowds: the one that cheered Sunday and the one that turned Friday.

## Lesson 2: Monday -- Jesus Clears the Temple

**Objective:** Kids will describe what made Jesus angry and why it mattered.

**Scripture:** Mark 11:15-19.

**Story:** Jesus walks into the temple courts and finds a marketplace -- money changers, animal sellers, noise where prayer should be. He drives them out and says, "My house will be called a house of prayer for all nations" (Mark 11:17). The court being used as a market was the one place non-Jewish worshipers were allowed to pray. Someone had turned the welcome mat into a checkout line.

**Discussion questions:**
- Is it ever okay to be angry? What made Jesus angry here?
- Who was getting shut out of worship because of the market?
- What "clutter" gets between us and God at church or at home?

**Activity:** Table flip, controlled. Set a table with junk -- coins, toy animals, receipts, phones -- and cover it with a cloth. Kids clear it item by item, naming one thing that distracts them from God as they remove each piece. Underneath: a sign reading "A house of prayer."

**Memory verse:** "My house will be called a house of prayer." -- Mark 11:17

**Age note:** Preschoolers hear it as "Jesus wanted His house to be for talking to God" and simply help tidy. Elementary kids can handle the fairness angle: the sellers were overcharging poor worshipers.

## Lesson 3: Tuesday -- The Greatest Commandment and the Widow's Coins

**Objective:** Kids will state the two greatest commandments and give an example of giving that costs something.

**Scripture:** Mark 12:28-34 and 12:41-44.

**Story:** On Tuesday Jesus taught all day. A teacher asks which commandment is greatest, and Jesus answers with two: love God with everything you are, and love your neighbor as yourself (Mark 12:30-31). Later, watching people drop offerings into the temple box, He points out a widow who gave two tiny coins -- and says she gave more than all the rich people, because they gave leftovers and she gave everything.

**Discussion questions:**
- Jesus was asked for one commandment and gave two. Why do you think they belong together?
- The widow's gift was worth almost nothing. Why did Jesus say it was the biggest?
- What's something you could give this week that would actually cost you a little?

**Activity:** Two coins. Give every child two paper coins. On one they write something they'll give God this week (time, kindness, an honest apology); on the other, something they'll give a neighbor. Drop them into a class offering box and read a few aloud.

**Memory verse:** "Love the Lord your God with all your heart." -- Mark 12:30

**Age note:** Preschoolers do the widow's coins only -- two real pennies into a jar, with the line "She gave her whole all." Elementary kids can debate the "leftovers versus first" question.

## Lesson 4: Wednesday -- The Perfume and the Betrayal

**Objective:** Kids will contrast two responses to Jesus: extravagant love and betrayal.

**Scripture:** Mark 14:1-11.

**Story:** At dinner in Bethany, a woman breaks open a jar of costly perfume and pours it over Jesus' head. People grumble about the waste. Jesus defends her: "She did what she could" (Mark 14:8). In the very next scene, Judas -- one of the twelve -- goes to the chief priests and offers to hand Jesus over for money. Same week, same Jesus, opposite hearts.

**Discussion questions:**
- Why did the others call the perfume a waste? Why didn't Jesus?
- What do you think Judas was hoping to get?
- If those two people are the two options, which one is easier to be? Which do you want to be?

**Activity:** Scent memory. Put a drop of a strong scent (vanilla, peppermint) on a cotton ball for each child to smell and take home in a small bag. Explain that the whole house smelled like her gift for days -- generous love leaves a trace people notice.

**Memory verse:** "She did what she could." -- Mark 14:8

**Age note:** Skip the betrayal thread entirely with preschoolers; teach only the beautiful gift. Elementary kids can hold both, and the contrast is what makes the lesson land.

## Lesson 5: Thursday -- The Last Supper and the Towel

**Objective:** Kids will explain the new command Jesus gave and demonstrate one way to serve someone.

**Scripture:** John 13:1-17 and Luke 22:14-20.

**Story:** In an upstairs room, Jesus takes off His outer robe, wraps a towel around His waist, and washes His friends' dusty feet -- the job of the lowest servant in the house. Then He shares bread and cup and says to remember Him. "A new command I give you: Love one another. As I have loved you, so you must love one another" (John 13:34).

**Discussion questions:**
- Why were the disciples so uncomfortable when Jesus knelt down?
- Jesus said, "I have set you an example" (John 13:15). An example of what?
- What's the "foot washing" job in your house or classroom -- the one nobody wants?

**Activity:** Service towels. Each child decorates a small white washcloth with fabric markers and writes one job they'll do for someone at home this week without being asked. Send the towel home as a reminder. (For a fuller retelling to use as your story segment, see [The Last Supper for kids](/blog/the-last-supper-for-kids).)

**Memory verse:** "Love one another. As I have loved you." -- John 13:34

**Age note:** Preschoolers wash a doll's feet in a warm basin -- they will do this for twenty minutes happily. Elementary kids should hear the detail that Jesus washed Judas's feet too.

## Lesson 6: Thursday Night -- The Garden of Gethsemane

**Objective:** Kids will identify that Jesus felt real sorrow and prayed honestly, and name one hard thing they can pray about.

**Scripture:** Matthew 26:36-46.

**Story:** After supper Jesus goes to a garden of olive trees to pray. He is deeply sad. He asks God three times to take the cup away if there is any other way, and each time He ends with, "Yet not as I will, but as you will" (Matthew 26:39). His friends fall asleep. Jesus chooses to go forward anyway.

**Discussion questions:**
- Is it okay to tell God you don't want something hard? What did Jesus do?
- Jesus asked His friends to stay awake with Him and they didn't. Has a friend ever let you down?
- What's the difference between "God, please change this" and "God, whatever You want"?

**Activity:** Olive garden prayer cards. Kids cut a simple olive-leaf shape, write or draw one hard thing on it, and add the words "Not my way -- Your way" on the back. Tape all the leaves to a paper tree and pray over the tree together.

**Memory verse:** "Yet not as I will, but as you will." -- Matthew 26:39

**Age note:** For preschoolers, one sentence is enough: "Jesus was very sad, so He talked to God about it -- and we can too." Elementary kids can sit with the harder truth that Jesus chose this.

## Lesson 7: Good Friday -- The Cross

**Objective:** Kids will state in their own words why Jesus died, without needing graphic detail.

**Scripture:** Luke 23:32-46 (read verses 33-34 and 44-46 aloud; summarize the rest).

**Story:** Tell it gently and truthfully. Jesus was arrested, put on trial by people who lied about Him, and sentenced to die on a cross even though He had done nothing wrong. He could have stopped it and He didn't. While it was happening He prayed for the people who did it: "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34). At the end He said, "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" (Luke 23:46). Then the whole land went dark. Stop there for a beat -- but do not end class there. Tell them plainly: "That is not the end of the story. Sunday is coming."

**Discussion questions:**
- Jesus had done nothing wrong. Why did He let this happen?
- He prayed for the people hurting Him. What does that show about Him?
- Why do Christians call this Friday "Good"?

**Activity:** Nail and cross. Give each child two craft sticks and twine to bind a cross, plus one small nail or nail-shaped paper cutout to tie on. Then hand out red hearts to cover the nail: "Jesus took what we deserved, and covered it with love." Every child leaves holding a cross with the nail hidden under a heart.

**Memory verse:** "God demonstrates his own love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us." -- Romans 5:8

**Age note:** Preschoolers get three sentences total, no nails, and a cross to color: "Jesus died. He did it because He loves us. Three days later He came back alive." Elementary kids can handle the trial, the injustice, and the word "forgive." For a longer age-appropriate retelling, use [The cross for kids](/blog/the-cross-for-kids), and for parent language, [how to explain Easter to a child](/blog/how-to-explain-easter-to-a-child) is the plainest script we have.

## Lesson 8: Saturday -- The Day of Waiting

**Objective:** Kids will describe what the disciples felt on Saturday and connect it to waiting in their own lives.

**Scripture:** Matthew 27:57-66.

**Story:** Nothing happens on Saturday. Jesus' body is in a borrowed tomb, a large stone is rolled across the entrance, guards are posted, and His friends sit in a locked room with no idea what tomorrow holds. This is the quietest day in the Bible. The disciples did not know the ending. We do.

**Discussion questions:**
- What's the hardest part of waiting when you don't know how something will turn out?
- The disciples thought the story was over. What did they not know yet?
- Where in your life are you in a "Saturday" -- still waiting?

**Activity:** Stone and tomb. Each child molds a small tomb from playdough or clay with a rolling stone door, closes it, and sets it aside without opening it. It stays closed until next week's lesson. The suspense is the point, and elementary kids feel it.

**Memory verse:** "Wait for the Lord; be strong and take heart." -- Psalm 27:14

**Age note:** Preschoolers do the tomb craft and the single line "Jesus' friends were sad, but not for long." Elementary kids can name their own waiting, which is where this lesson does its real work.

## Lesson 9: Easter Sunday -- The Empty Tomb

**Objective:** Kids will state the central Christian claim: Jesus was dead, and He is alive.

**Scripture:** Luke 24:1-12 and John 20:11-18.

**Story:** Before sunrise, women carry spices to the tomb, worrying the whole way about who will move the stone. They arrive and the stone is already rolled back. The tomb is empty. Angels ask, "Why do you look for the living among the dead? He is not here; he has risen!" (Luke 24:5-6). Mary lingers outside, crying, and mistakes Jesus for the gardener -- until He says one word: her name (John 20:16). Then she knows.

**Discussion questions:**
- The women went to the tomb sad and worried. What changed everything?
- Mary didn't recognize Jesus until He said her name. Why do you think that's the detail John kept?
- If Jesus is really alive, what difference does that make on a normal Wednesday?

**Activity:** Open the tombs. Bring back last week's clay tombs and let every child roll the stone away to find the tomb empty -- then shout the line together: "He is not here; He has risen!" Follow with resurrection eggs if you have them, or an empty-tomb diorama from our companion list of [Easter crafts for Sunday school](/blog/easter-crafts-for-sunday-school).

**Memory verse:** "He is not here; he has risen!" -- Luke 24:6

**Age note:** Preschoolers can carry this whole lesson -- it is the most preschool-friendly moment of Holy Week. Elementary kids can add the evidence angle: the guards, the folded cloths, the many witnesses.

## Lesson 10: After Easter -- Burning Hearts and a Sending

**Objective:** Kids will describe what the risen Jesus asked His followers to do next.

**Scripture:** Luke 24:13-35 and Matthew 28:16-20.

**Story:** Two friends walk to Emmaus, heartbroken, and a stranger joins them and explains the whole Bible on the road. They only recognize Him when He breaks the bread -- and then He's gone. "Were not our hearts burning within us while he talked with us on the road?" (Luke 24:32). Later Jesus meets the eleven on a mountain and sends them out with a promise: "Surely I am with you always, to the very end of the age" (Matthew 28:20).

**Discussion questions:**
- The friends were sad because they didn't know the ending yet. Who do you know who needs to hear it?
- What do you think "hearts burning" means?
- Jesus' last words were a promise, not a goodbye. What did He promise?

**Activity:** Road to Emmaus walk-and-talk. Take the class on a slow lap of the building in pairs; each pair retells the Easter story to each other before returning. Finish by writing one name on a card -- someone they could tell this week -- and putting it in their pocket.

**Memory verse:** "Surely I am with you always, to the very end of the age." -- Matthew 28:20

**Age note:** Preschoolers do the walk and repeat "Jesus is alive!" the whole way. Elementary kids can name real people and pray for them by name.

## How Do You Fit 10 Lessons Into Holy Week?

Most classes get two Sundays and maybe one midweek service, so treat these as a menu, not a mandate. The usual pattern: teach Lesson 1 on Palm Sunday, Lesson 5 or 7 at a midweek Maundy Thursday or Good Friday service, and Lesson 9 on Easter morning. Then use Lessons 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, and 10 in the weeks after Easter, when attendance settles and you have room to slow down -- Holy Week does not expire on Monday.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you explain the crucifixion to young children?

Say it in three short sentences and then move to Sunday: "Jesus died on the cross to take the punishment for our sins. He chose to do it because He loves us. Three days later God raised Him back to life." Skip physical detail entirely for kids under about seven -- the theology is age-appropriate, the imagery is not. Watch faces, answer the question actually asked, and always finish the class on the resurrection.

### What order should I teach these Easter lessons in?

Teach them in the numbered order, which follows Holy Week day by day from Palm Sunday through the resurrection appearances. If you only have two Sundays, teach Lesson 1 (Palm Sunday) and Lesson 9 (the empty tomb), and use the others in the weeks after Easter. The sequence matters more than the completeness -- kids need Friday to come before Sunday.

### Are these Easter Sunday school lessons free to use?

Yes -- every lesson here is free to print, copy, and teach in your class, and none of them require buying curriculum. Supplies are standard supply-closet items: construction paper, craft sticks, twine, playdough, washcloths, and paper coins. Total cost from scratch is under $30 for a class of fifteen.

### What age are these lessons written for?

The main text is written for elementary kids ages 6-11, and every lesson includes an Age note telling you exactly what to cut or simplify for preschoolers ages 3-5. For preschool classes, use only the story sentence, the activity, and the memory verse -- drop the discussion questions and the betrayal, trial, and waiting threads.

### How long should an Easter Sunday school lesson take?

Plan 40 minutes: 10 for the story, 10 for discussion, 15 for the activity, and 5 for the memory verse and prayer. Easter Sunday itself usually runs shorter because of packed services and visiting families, so on that morning cut the discussion to two questions and protect the activity -- it's the part kids will tell their parents about.

### How do I teach Easter to kids who have visitors or no church background?

Assume nothing and define the words as you go: cross, tomb, resurrection, sin, forgive. Easter Sunday is the highest-visitor morning of the year, so tell the story as if no one has heard it, and keep the invitation simple rather than pressured. A visiting child who leaves knowing "Jesus died and came back alive because He loves people" has received the whole message.
