A devotional is the shortest complete form in Christian writing: one passage of Scripture, one true moment from a life, one connection between them, and one thing to do about it, all in the space of 300 words. That compression is why editors reject most of what they receive, and why the writers who learn the form can sell it reliably. The two biggest devotional markets both pay $30 a piece and publish every single day of the year; they need good writers more than good writers need them.

This guide covers the structure editors actually screen for, the word counts each market wants, a worked example, the mistakes that get pieces returned, and exactly where to submit, with every market's details verified on its own guidelines page in November 2026.

## The Four-Part Shape Every Devotional Shares

Markets name the parts differently, but nearly every published devotional walks the same line. Christian Devotions, one of the outlets below, states it outright in their guidelines as **Hook, Book, Look, Took**, and it is a useful map for all of them:

1. **Hook: the moment.** A specific, true scene from ordinary life. Not "we all face trials"; instead, the dishwasher flooding the kitchen the morning of the job interview. Specificity is the whole trick: readers trust details.
2. **Book: the passage.** One Scripture, and only one. Christian Devotions makes the one-passage rule explicit, and the other markets enforce it by editing. The verse should not merely decorate the story; the story should need the verse.
3. **Look: the connection.** Two or three sentences where the moment and the passage turn out to be about the same thing. This is the part that is actually hard, and it is where editors decide.
4. **Took: the application.** One concrete thing the reader can do, pray, or notice today. Not three things. One.

A prayer line or a "thought for the day" often closes the piece, depending on the market's house format.

## Word Counts by Market

The form is tight and the ceilings are real:

| Market | Length | Shape notes (from their guidelines) |
|---|---|---|
| The Upper Room | 350-400 words max | One point, one personal experience, one passage |
| Keys for Kids | ~350 word story + 50-65 word application | Story for ages 6-12 built on an everyday object |
| Christian Devotions | 300-400 words | Hook, Book, Look, Took; one Scripture only |

Draft at 450 and cut. A devotional almost never fails for being too short.

## A Worked Example (About 120 Words of It)

Here is the skeleton of the form doing its job:

> My daughter's nightlight burned out on the one night she most wanted it: the first night in the new house. I sat on the floor of a room full of boxes while she catalogued everything the dark might contain. I could not argue her out of a single fear. All I could do was stay, and eventually the staying was the answer.
>
> "Even the darkness is not dark to you; the night is bright as the day" (Psalm 139:12).
>
> I keep asking God for a working nightlight: the visible fix, the proof. What He keeps offering instead is the staying. Tonight, name the dark thing you have been cataloguing, and ask Him to sit on the floor of it with you.

Notice what it does not do: no second illustration, no survey of what commentators say about Psalm 139, no three-point application. One moment, one verse, one turn, one ask.

## The Mistakes That Get Devotionals Returned

- **Starting with the lesson instead of the moment.** If your first sentence could open a sermon, cut it and start where something happened.
- **The generic anecdote.** "A woman once faced a hard season" is not a story; the markets above all ask for first-person, specific, true.
- **Verse stacking.** Three supporting references read as padding at this length, and at least one market's guidelines prohibit them outright.
- **Preaching the application.** "We all must learn to trust God more" commands; "name the dark thing tonight" invites. Editors can tell.
- **AI drafting.** The Upper Room's guidelines say plainly that they do not publish AI-generated content, and Keys for Kids excludes stories written fully or partly with AI. A devotional is 300 words of your actual life; outsource it and there is nothing left.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a devotional be?

250-400 words for adult markets (The Upper Room caps at 400; Christian Devotions wants 300-400), and for children about 350 words of story plus a 50-65 word application (Keys for Kids). Church booklet devotionals usually run the same length.

### Do devotional writers get paid?

At the two biggest daily markets, yes: The Upper Room and Keys for Kids each pay $30 per accepted piece, per their current guidelines. Many ministry sites publish devotionals unpaid. Treat the unpaid tier as training and clips, then sell to the paying markets.

### What Bible translation should I use?

Whatever the market's guidelines name, which is part of why you read them first. Keys for Kids works mostly in the NKJV per third-party listings, Christian Devotions defaults to NIV if you do not say otherwise, and every market wants the translation noted.

### Can I use AI to help write a devotional?

Not for the writing itself if you want to publish: The Upper Room and Keys for Kids both exclude AI-written content explicitly. The form is a true story from your life connected to Scripture, which is the one thing a model cannot supply.
