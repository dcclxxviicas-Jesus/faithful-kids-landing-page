In September 2026, an op-ed from our founder ran in The Christian Post: a piece about taking his kids' screens away and why it didn't work. We are a small company nobody at that newsroom had heard of. The piece ran anyway, because op-ed pages do not buy credentials, they buy a specific argument told by the person who earned the right to make it.

That is the entire game, and it is learnable. This guide covers what an op-ed actually is, the structure editors scan for, how to pitch one, and where Christian writers can realistically place one, including with us.

## What an Op-Ed Is (and Is Not)

An op-ed is a short argument, usually 600-800 words, written by someone outside the publication, that takes one clear position on something people currently care about. The name is old newspaper geography: the page opposite the editorials.

What it is not: a personal essay (no argument), a sermon (wrong authority; you are persuading readers who did not ask to be taught), a press release (editors delete these on sight), or a survey of a topic (a survey takes no position). The fastest self-test: can you state your piece as one sentence someone could disagree with? "Screen-time rules matter" fails that test. "Taking the screen away is not a plan" passes, which is why that one became both the op-ed and, later, a [featured essay in our parenting section](/parenting).

## The Anatomy Editors Scan For

Op-ed editors read dozens of submissions a week and decide in the first paragraph. The working structure:

1. **The lede: a concrete moment or fact, not a throat-clear.** Our Christian Post piece opened inside the actual experiment of a screen-free household, not with "In today's digital age." Any sentence that could open a thousand other pieces gets cut.
2. **The thesis, by paragraph two.** One arguable sentence. The reader should be able to quote your position without reading further.
3. **The earned authority line.** One or two sentences on why you, specifically: the parent who ran the experiment, the Sunday school teacher of fifteen years, the pastor who buried the church's oldest member and baptized her great-granddaughter in the same month. Lived credentials beat titles on an op-ed page.
4. **Two or three pieces of evidence**, at least one of which is a story only you can tell. Statistics support; stories persuade.
5. **The turn.** Address the strongest version of the other side in one honest paragraph. Pieces that pretend no counterargument exists read as propaganda, and religious writers are stereotyped as doing exactly this, so doing the opposite stands out.
6. **The close that lands on action or reframe.** Not a summary. The last line should be the second-most quotable sentence in the piece.

Keep it 600-800 words. If the outlet wants different, their guidelines will say.

## How to Pitch One

- **Finished piece beats pitch for unknown writers.** A known columnist can sell an idea; the rest of us sell the finished 700 words. Send the full text in the email body (most op-ed desks dislike attachments from strangers) with a two-sentence note: who you are, why now.
- **The "why now" is half the sale.** Tie the piece to something current: a season, a study, a cultural moment. The same argument that is ignored in March gets published in December if it reads through an Advent lens.
- **One outlet at a time, with a stated deadline.** Standard practice is to tell the editor you will take the piece elsewhere if you have not heard back in a week or two; op-ed desks expect this and it is not rude.
- **Expect silence, not rejection letters.** Send, wait out your window, move down your list. The piece that ran for us was not placed on the first attempt at anything in this niche; persistence is a feature of the system, not a sign of failure.

## Where Christian Writers Can Place Op-Eds

- **The Christian Post** runs outside contributors in its Voices section; that is where ours ran. Large reach, actively publishes non-staff writers.
- **Plough** considers idea-driven pieces by pitch to submissions@plough.com, though their own guidelines say narrative-driven essays are the preferred shape; an op-ed for Plough should wear its argument inside a story.
- **Reformed Perspective** pays $100-$300 for cultural commentary answering "why should Christians care, and what might they do," for a conservatively Reformed readership.
- **Your local newspaper.** Genuinely underrated: local op-ed pages are hungry, a faith angle on a local issue is distinctive there, and a local clip is a real credential for the national outlets.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should an op-ed be?

600-800 words is the standard band across newspapers and digital outlets. Write to 700 and let the outlet's own guidelines override.

### Do you get paid for op-eds?

Usually not at newspapers; the currency is reach and credibility. In the Christian space, Reformed Perspective's $100-$300 honorarium for commentary is the notable paying exception among outlets with current public guidelines.

### What makes an op-ed different from a blog post?

An op-ed argues one contestable position for a general audience under an editor's standards; a blog post can wander, assume a friendly reader, and skip the counterargument. The discipline of the form (thesis by paragraph two, the turn, the earned-authority line) is what op-ed editors are screening for.

### Can a pastor or teacher write an op-ed without being a professional writer?

That is who op-ed pages exist for: practitioners with earned, specific authority. A youth pastor who has watched ten years of phones enter the youth room has more standing on screen-time than most professional columnists, provided the piece argues one clear position.
