Some of the most-watched channels on all of YouTube belong to children. Three of them, Vlad and Niki, Kids Diana Show and Like Nastya, each have more than 130 million subscribers, more than most of the biggest adult creators on the platform. If you have a child under ten, there's a good chance one of them is already in your house.

This guide covers who the biggest kid influencers are, the wave of state laws written to protect the children in those videos, what the research says about kids who watch them, and channels worth putting in their place. Every subscriber count below was read from the channel's own YouTube page on November 18, 2026, and every law from its official text or legislature record.

## The Short Version

- **The biggest kid influencers:** Vlad and Niki (151 million subscribers), Kids Diana Show (138 million) and Like Nastya (134 million), followed by Toys and Colors, Sierra & Rhia FAM and Ryan's World.
- **The law is catching up:** at least eight states now require part of a child creator's earnings to be set aside for them, starting with Illinois in 2024. Minnesota bans paid content creation by children under 14.
- **The documented harms are real:** Netflix's *Bad Influence* (April 2025), a $1.85 million settlement by 11 former child creators, and the Ruby Franke case.
- **For viewers, the clearest evidence is about marketing:** in the most-watched kid influencer videos studied, 90% of the food shown was unhealthy branded products.
- **Better options exist:** BibleProject, Superbook and Saddleback Kids on the faith side; Mark Rober, Wild Kratts and SciShow Kids for curiosity.

## How Kid Influencers Make Money, and Why the Law Got Involved

A kid influencer channel earns the way any big channel does: ads, brand deals, merchandise and licensing. The difference is who is doing the work. Until recently, a child could appear in videos earning millions with no legal right to any of it, because child-performer protections were written for film and TV, not for a parent filming at home.

That changed fast. Most of the new laws share a common test: a child counts when they appear in at least 30% of a creator's paid video content in a 30-day period. Here is where things stand, from each law's official text or legislature record:

| State (law) | In effect | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| Illinois (SB 1782) | July 1, 2024 | Covers children under 16; a share of earnings must go into a trust the child can claim at 18, with records kept. The first law of its kind. |
| California (SB 764) | January 1, 2025 | Creators who feature minors in at least 30% of their content must set aside a share of earnings in trust for the child. |
| Minnesota (Minn. Stat. 181A.13) | July 1, 2025 | Children under 14 may not be paid content creators; older featured minors get a trust share. |
| Utah (HB 322) | May 7, 2025 | Applies once a creator earns at least $150,000 a year from social media; roughly half the child's share of earnings goes to the child. |
| Montana (HB 392) | Passed 2025 | Tiered trust share of 10%, 25% or 50%, depending on how much of the content features the child. |
| Virginia (Va. Code 40.1-109.1) | Passed 2025 | Covers children under 16; at least half their share of earnings in trust. |
| Arkansas (Act 982 of 2025) | July 1, 2026 | Covers minors up to 17 once the creator earns at least $15,000 a year; trust set-aside available at 18. |
| Tennessee (Public Chapter 687 of 2026) | July 1, 2026 | Trust set-aside, records, and deletion of content on request. An under-14 ban in the original bill was removed before passage. |

California went further in September 2026 with **SB 1247**, signed September 30, which lets an adult who was featured as a child in paid content ask for those videos to be deleted or edited.

On the platform side, YouTube paid a record **$170 million** in 2019 to settle Federal Trade Commission and New York charges that it collected children's data without parental consent. That settlement is why videos marked "made for kids" now have no comments, no personalized ads, no notification bell and no autoplay on the home screen. YouTube also requires creators to be **16 or older to live stream**; younger children can appear only if an adult is visibly present.

## The Dark Side: What the Documentaries Showed

Most parents first heard the word "kidfluencing" from one of these:

- **Bad Influence: The Dark Side of Kidfluencing** (Netflix, April 9, 2025) is a three-part series about Piper Rockelle, her group of child creators called "the Squad," and her mother and manager, Tiffany Smith. In October 2024, 11 former Squad members who had sued Smith and her boyfriend, the channel's director, settled for **$1.85 million**, according to NBC News. Smith denied wrongdoing as part of the settlement.
- **The Ruby Franke case.** Franke, whose family vlog *8 Passengers* had more than 2 million subscribers before it was shut down, pleaded guilty to four counts of aggravated child abuse and was sentenced in February 2024 to up to 30 years. Hulu released a docuseries about it in February 2025.
- **A 2024 New York Times investigation** of about 5,000 parent-run Instagram accounts for young girls found that the larger accounts drew a growing share of adult male followers.

None of this means every family channel is abusive. It does mean the incentives are strong and, until recently, almost no one was watching.

## What the Research Says About Kids Who Watch

- **Children in videos draw more views.** Pew Research Center's 2019 study of popular YouTube channels found that videos featuring a child who appeared to be under 13 got nearly **three times as many views** on average as other videos.
- **Almost every young child is watching.** In Pew's 2020 survey, **80%** of parents of a child 11 or younger said their child watches YouTube, and **46%** said their child had come across videos inappropriate for their age.
- **The marketing works on kids.** In a randomized trial published in *Pediatrics* (2019), children 9 to 11 who saw influencers with unhealthy snacks ate significantly more calories afterwards than children who saw influencers with non-food products.
- **The food is overwhelmingly junk.** An NYU study in *Pediatrics* (2020) looked at the five most-watched kid influencers of 2019: **90.34%** of the food and drink shown in their videos was unhealthy branded items, in videos viewed more than 48 billion times. A 2023 study of 400 child-influencer videos found branded products appeared 592 times, and just one video disclosed a food sponsorship.

What we couldn't find is a peer-reviewed study of how being a kid influencer affects the child creator. The evidence there so far is legal and journalistic, not scientific, and it's honest to say so.

## What Parents Can Do

1. **Watch a few videos yourself first.** Five minutes tells you more than a channel's subscriber count.
2. **Look for the "made for kids" label.** On YouTube it switches off comments and personalized ads, which removes two of the riskier parts of the platform.
3. **Talk about the ads.** Ask your child, "Why do you think they're showing that toy?"
4. **Use YouTube Kids or a supervised account** for younger children, and turn off autoplay.
5. **Replace, don't just remove.** Taking a favorite channel away works better when something good fills the gap.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who is the biggest kid influencer on YouTube?

Vlad and Niki, with 151 million subscribers on November 18, 2026, followed by Kids Diana Show (138 million) and Like Nastya (134 million).

### What is a kidfluencer?

A child who earns money from social media, through ads, brand deals or merchandise, usually on a channel run by a parent. The term covers both children with their own channels and children featured heavily in a family vlog.

### Are there laws protecting child influencers?

Yes. Illinois was first, in July 2024, and by 2026 at least eight states (Illinois, California, Minnesota, Utah, Montana, Virginia, Arkansas and Tennessee) require part of a child creator's earnings to be set aside in trust. Minnesota also bans paid content creation by children under 14, and California's SB 1247 lets adults request deletion of videos they appeared in as children.

### Is it bad for kids to watch kid influencers?

Not automatically, but the research flags marketing: in the most-watched kid influencer videos, 90% of the food shown was unhealthy branded products, and children who see influencers with snacks eat more of them. Watching with your child and choosing channels deliberately makes the biggest difference.

### What is Bad Influence on Netflix about?

*Bad Influence: The Dark Side of Kidfluencing* (April 2025) is a three-part documentary about YouTuber Piper Rockelle, her group of child creators, and allegations against her mother and manager. Eleven former members settled a lawsuit for $1.85 million in 2024.
