## How to Use a Nativity Coloring Series

Do one scene per day through December, in order. Twenty scenes and twenty-four days leaves room for the busy nights, and by Christmas Eve your child has colored the story from Gabriel to Egypt with the manger falling right where it belongs -- in the middle, not at the end.

Three ways families run it:

- **Advent chain.** One page a day, taped up in a row along a hallway. The growing strip *is* the countdown.
- **Sunday school series.** Four weeks, five scenes per week, with the class narrating the row back at the end.
- **Christmas Eve booklet.** Color all twenty across December, staple them in order, and read the finished book aloud on Christmas Eve. Kids will keep this one.

## Before the Birth (Pages 1-5)

The setup scenes almost every coloring set skips, which is a shame -- they're where the story's suspense lives.

**1. Gabriel Visits Zechariah (Luke 1:5-25)**
An old priest standing beside a golden incense altar inside the temple, smoke rising, an angel appearing at the right side of the altar. Zechariah's face shows shock. Lots of temple detail for older kids to fill in.

**2. The Angel Visits Mary (Luke 1:26-38)**
A young woman in a simple home, turning toward a tall angel standing in the doorway light. Keep her posture calm rather than frightened -- the scene ends with her saying yes. This is the most important page in the set.

**3. Mary Visits Elizabeth (Luke 1:39-45)**
Two women embracing at a doorway in the hill country, hills stepping back behind them, a water jar set down on the ground. A warm, quiet page and a good one for younger colorers.

**4. Mary's Song (Luke 1:46-55)**
Mary with hands lifted, the words "My soul magnifies the Lord" (Luke 1:46) lettered in an arc above her, with a border of stars and small musical notes. Half coloring page, half verse page.

**5. Joseph's Dream (Matthew 1:20-24)**
Joseph asleep on a mat with an angel standing over him, a window showing a night sky. Draw the dream as a soft cloud shape floating above his head. Kids get this visual instantly.

## The Journey and the Birth (Pages 6-9)

**6. The Road to Bethlehem (Luke 2:4-5)**
Mary on a donkey with Joseph walking alongside, a winding road, low hills, a town on the horizon. The Bible doesn't mention a donkey, so tell kids that part is the artist's guess -- an easy, useful lesson in what Scripture says versus what tradition pictures.

**7. No Room (Luke 2:7)**
A closed wooden door with a small window and a crowded street behind, Joseph knocking. Warm light spills from the windows above and none of it reaches them. The most emotionally powerful page in the set for older kids.

**8. Laid in a Manger (Luke 2:7)**
The central scene: a wooden feeding trough lined with straw, the baby wrapped in cloths, Mary and Joseph kneeling on either side. Keep the manger large and the straw lines wide -- this page gets colored slowly.

**9. The Stable Animals (traditional)**
An ox, a donkey, sheep, and doves in the rafters, all looking toward the manger. This is tradition rather than text, and it's the page toddlers love most. Say so cheerfully and let them color it anyway.

## The Shepherds (Pages 10-13)

Four pages that work beautifully as a single strip, because the shepherds' scene moves: field, angel, host, town.

**10. Shepherds in the Fields at Night (Luke 2:8)**
A dark hillside, a small fire, sheep scattered around, three shepherds sitting and watching. Encourage dark blues and purples for the sky so the next page's light pops.

**11. The Angel Appears (Luke 2:9-12)**
The same hillside, now blazing. One angel above, radiant lines shooting outward, shepherds shielding their eyes and falling back. Draw the light rays wide and empty so kids can leave them white or fill them gold.

**12. The Heavenly Host (Luke 2:13-14)**
The sky filled with angels, "Glory to God in the highest" (Luke 2:14) lettered across the top. Draw a dozen angels of varying sizes so the page reads as a crowd, not a lineup.

**13. The Shepherds Hurry to Bethlehem (Luke 2:15-16)**
Shepherds running down a hill toward town with staffs in hand and sheep trailing behind. Motion lines make this one fun. A companion page can show them telling everyone afterward (Luke 2:17-20).

## At the Temple (Pages 14-15)

The Christmas story's overlooked chapter, and two lovely pages.

**14. Simeon Holds the Baby (Luke 2:25-35)**
An old man in the temple courts cradling the infant, Mary and Joseph beside him with two doves in a small cage. His face is joy, not sorrow. Pair with Luke 2:30 lettered underneath.

**15. Anna the Prophetess (Luke 2:36-38)**
A very old woman with hands raised in thanks, temple pillars behind her, people gathered to listen. Rarely drawn, and worth adding -- kids remember the pages nobody else has.

## The Wise Men (Pages 16-19)

Note the timeline as you color: Matthew places the visit at a house, not the stable, and never says how many wise men there were. Coloring these as separate pages from the manger is the easiest way to teach that without a lecture.

**16. The Star (Matthew 2:2)**
A single large star over a distant town, with a long tail of light pointing down. Simple, striking, and perfect for a child who wants one big shape to fill.

**17. Wise Men Traveling (Matthew 2:1-2)**
Camels crossing a night desert, riders looking up at the star, dunes in soft layered bands. Great for a child who likes drawing animals.

**18. Before King Herod (Matthew 2:3-8)**
A throne room with a scowling king, wise men standing before him, scribes with open scrolls at the side. This page adds the danger the story actually has, which older kids find far more interesting than another stable.

**19. The Gifts (Matthew 2:11)**
Inside a house, the wise men kneeling before the child with Mary, three ornate gift containers open on the floor -- gold, frankincense, myrrh. Label the gifts so the words get learned along with the picture.

## After (Page 20)

**20. The Flight to Egypt (Matthew 2:13-15)**
A family leaving in darkness, Joseph leading, an angel behind them, pyramids faint on the far horizon. It's the page that reminds kids the story kept going after Christmas morning -- and it makes the manger scene land harder in hindsight.

## How to Turn a Nativity Coloring Page Into a Lesson

The page is the excuse; the conversation over the crayons is the teaching. A simple rhythm that works every time:

1. **Read the two or three verses first, before any coloring.** Short passage, out loud, then hand over the page.
2. **Ask one question about one detail.** "Why do you think the angel said 'do not be afraid' first?" "What would you have done if you were Joseph?" One question, not a quiz.
3. **Narrate while they color.** This is when to add context -- what a manger actually was, why shepherds were an odd choice of first audience, how far Bethlehem was from Nazareth.
4. **Ask them to retell it at the end.** "Tell me this page." Retelling is where retention forms, and a child holding their own drawing retells much more willingly than a child holding nothing.
5. **Add each finished page to the row.** Seeing the story grow left to right teaches sequence, which is the single hardest thing about the Christmas narrative for young kids.

One more thing worth doing: keep the wise men pages physically separated from the manger pages on the wall, with the temple scenes in between. Kids absorb the real chronology from the layout without you ever having to correct the nativity set on the mantel. ## Printing Tips for Christmas Coloring Pages

**Use 24 lb paper or light card stock.** Standard 20 lb copy paper bleeds through the second a marker touches it, and December pages often become gifts or ornaments that need to survive handling.

**Print grayscale in draft or economy mode.** These are outlines. Color ink on black line art is wasted, and draft mode roughly halves ink use with no visible difference.

**Batch the whole set in one November print run.** Twenty pages printed on a calm evening in November beats twenty separate "I need to print something" moments in December, which is exactly when the printer runs dry.

**Print the star and angel pages on slightly heavier stock** if you plan to cut them into ornaments or a garland. Punch a hole, add ribbon, hang the finished story on the tree.

**Scale to 85 percent for a booklet.** Smaller pages staple into a keepsake book that fits a bookshelf, and a smaller area is less daunting for a five-year-old on day 14.

**Laminate one manger scene and hand over dry-erase markers** for the child who wants to color it every single day. One print, a whole season.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What scenes should a nativity coloring set include?

At minimum: the angel visiting Mary, the journey to Bethlehem, the birth in the manger, the shepherds and the angels, and the wise men with their gifts. A fuller set adds Gabriel's visit to Zechariah, Joseph's dream, Simeon and Anna at the temple, and the flight to Egypt. Twenty scenes covers Luke 1-2 and Matthew 1-2 nearly completely.

### What age are nativity coloring pages best for?

Ages 3 to 10, with the complexity shifting. Under 5, use single large figures -- one angel, one star, one sheep. Ages 6 to 8 handle full scenes with several figures. Ages 9 and up enjoy detailed pages like the throne room or the heavenly host, and often prefer to draw their own version of a scene you describe.

### Were there really three wise men at the manger?

Matthew names three gifts -- gold, frankincense, and myrrh -- but never says how many wise men brought them, and he places their visit at a house, not the stable (Matthew 2:11). Traditional nativity sets combine the shepherds and the wise men into one scene for convenience. Coloring them as separate pages is a gentle, memorable way to show kids the difference between what the text says and what art has added.

### How do I use these pages as an Advent countdown?

Number the pages 1 through 20 and color one per day starting December 5, which lands the final page on Christmas Eve. Tape each finished sheet in a row along a hallway or across a doorway, so the story grows visibly through the month. The gaps left for busy nights are a feature, not a failure.

### Can I use nativity coloring pages in a church Christmas program?

Yes, and they work especially well as an arrival activity while families are still being seated. Print the manger and star pages, set them out with crayons, and the room stays calm through the last ten minutes of stragglers. Finished pages taped along the back wall also make a free, kid-made decoration for the program itself.
