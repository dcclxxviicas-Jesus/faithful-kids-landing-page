The best resurrection activities for kids are the ones where the craft itself carries the lesson -- an egg that is empty on purpose, a roll that comes out hollow, a stone too heavy to move. Here are 15 of them, each with a materials list, what to do, and the exact teaching moment to say out loud while your kids' hands are busy.

Most of these cost under five dollars and use things already in your kitchen. They are sorted roughly by how much setup they need, starting with the classic. If you are running a Sunday school class rather than a family, activities 1, 3, 9, 12, and 13 scale to a group of twenty without extra work.

## 1. Resurrection Eggs (The Full 12-Item List)

**Materials:** A dozen plastic eggs in a carton, a permanent marker to number them 1-12, and twelve small objects.

Number the eggs and fill them in order. Open one a day for twelve days before Easter, or all twelve in one sitting on Easter Eve. Read the verse, pull out the object, and let your child guess what part of the story it belongs to before you tell them.

1. **A small toy donkey or a leaf** -- Palm Sunday. Jesus rides into Jerusalem while crowds wave branches. *(Matthew 21:1-11)*
2. **Three dimes or silver coins** -- Judas agrees to hand Jesus over for thirty pieces of silver. *(Matthew 26:14-16)*
3. **A tiny piece of bread or a small cup** -- the Last Supper. "This is my body given for you." *(Luke 22:19)*
4. **A pair of praying hands cut from paper, or an olive** -- Gethsemane, where Jesus prayed "not my will, but yours." *(Luke 22:39-44)*
5. **A short piece of rope or leather cord** -- Jesus is arrested, bound, and led away. *(Matthew 27:1-2)*
6. **A few thorns from a rose stem, or a toothpick crown** -- the soldiers mock Jesus with a crown of thorns. *(Matthew 27:29)*
7. **A small cross made from two toothpicks and thread** -- Jesus carries His cross to Golgotha. *(John 19:17)*
8. **A nail** -- Jesus is crucified. Handle this one carefully with little ones and keep it brief. *(John 20:25)*
9. **Two dice** -- the soldiers cast lots for His clothing, exactly as Psalm 22:18 said they would. *(John 19:23-24)*
10. **A cotton ball with a drop of clove or myrrh oil, or a scrap of white linen** -- Jesus' body is wrapped in linen with spices. *(John 19:39-40)*
11. **A small stone** -- the stone rolled across the tomb, and sealed with a guard posted. *(Matthew 27:60-66)*
12. **Nothing. The egg is empty.** -- "He is not here; he has risen, just as he said." *(Matthew 28:6)*

**Teaching moment:** Say nothing when you open egg 12. Let them look inside, look at you, and figure it out. Kids remember the pause. Then read Matthew 28:6 and let them keep the empty egg in their room until Easter.

## 2. The Empty Tomb Garden

**Materials:** A shallow dish or pot, potting soil, a small terracotta pot or paper cup laid on its side, a flat stone that covers the opening, moss or grass seed, three twigs and string for crosses.

Fill the dish with soil. Press the small pot on its side into the soil to form a cave, pile soil over the top to make a hill, and set the stone in front of the opening. Plant grass seed or press moss over the hill, and stick three twig crosses on top. Water it and set it in a window.

Keep the stone in place through Good Friday and Saturday. On Easter morning, before your kids wake, roll the stone aside.

**Teaching moment:** The grass seed sprouting over a tomb is the whole point of Easter in one dish -- new life growing right on top of a grave. *(John 19:41; Matthew 28:2)*

## 3. Roll Away the Stone

**Materials:** A heavy object -- a full watering can, a bag of flour, a large rock -- and a stopwatch.

Ask each child to move the object across the room as fast as they can. Time them. Then explain that the stone at Jesus' tomb weighed somewhere between one and two tons, set in a groove, with Roman soldiers guarding it. Ask how long the women at the tomb would have needed to move it. The answer is never.

**Teaching moment:** The women walked to the tomb asking each other, "Who will roll the stone away for us?" (Mark 16:3). They had a problem with no solution -- and God had already solved it before they arrived. That is worth naming out loud for a child who is worrying about something.

## 4. Resurrection Rolls

**Materials:** A tube of crescent roll dough, large marshmallows, melted butter, cinnamon sugar.

Give each child a marshmallow -- that is Jesus' body. Roll it in melted butter and cinnamon sugar (the burial spices), then wrap it completely in a crescent triangle (the linen cloths), sealing every edge. Place the wrapped rolls in the pan (the tomb) and bake per the package directions.

When they come out, break one open. The marshmallow is gone. The roll is hollow.

**Teaching moment:** Ask what happened before you explain the science. The empty roll makes the empty tomb physical, and the smell of it becomes an Easter-morning memory. *(John 19:40; Luke 24:3)*

## 5. Overnight Resurrection Cookies

**Materials:** 1 cup pecans in a zip bag, a wooden spoon, 1 tsp vinegar, 3 egg whites, a pinch of salt, 1 cup sugar, a mixer, parchment, tape.

Make these on Saturday evening. Crush the pecans with the spoon (the soldiers beat Jesus). Smell the vinegar before adding it (He was offered vinegar on the cross, John 19:29). Add the egg whites, which represent life. Let each child taste a pinch of salt, and talk about the tears of His friends. Add the sugar -- God's love and sweetness. Beat until stiff peaks form, spoon onto parchment as rough little tomb shapes, put them in a 300F oven, then turn the oven OFF and tape the door shut. The tomb is sealed.

Go to bed. In the morning, break the seal. The cookies are hollow and cracked open.

**Teaching moment:** The going-to-bed part is the lesson. The disciples spent one long night with no answers, and the answer was already coming. *(Matthew 27:66; Matthew 28:1)*

## 6. Sunrise Breakfast Tradition

**Materials:** Whatever your family loves, cooked outside if you can, plus a blanket and one Bible.

Set an alarm early enough to be outside as the sky changes on Easter morning. Eat breakfast first, then read Matthew 28:1-10 while it is happening -- "at dawn on the first day of the week." Some families add a candle lit at the exact moment the sun clears the horizon.

**Teaching moment:** The resurrection was discovered at sunrise by people who had cried all weekend. Doing this outdoors once a year gives your kids a physical memory attached to the story, and it is the tradition families report keeping longest. Add a second layer as they get older: read John 21:9-14, where the risen Jesus cooks His friends breakfast on a beach.

## 7. Butterfly Life-Cycle Craft

**Materials:** A toilet-paper tube or a paper cocoon, tissue paper, pipe cleaners, and if you can manage it, a live caterpillar kit.

Make a caterpillar, wrap it in a cocoon, and set it aside on Good Friday. On Easter morning, open the cocoon to reveal a tissue-paper butterfly. With a live kit, the timing rarely lines up perfectly, and that is fine -- the waiting is part of it.

**Teaching moment:** A caterpillar goes into a dark, sealed place and comes out as something that flies. Paul uses this exact logic in 1 Corinthians 15:42-44: what is buried is not what comes out. This is the gentlest way to talk about resurrection with a child who has lost a grandparent or a pet.

## 8. Plant a Seed on Good Friday

**Materials:** Fast-sprouting seeds (beans, sunflowers, or grass), a cup, soil, a marker.

Have each child bury a seed on Good Friday and write the date on the cup. Talk about how burying something usually means it is gone. Water it every day and watch what happens over the next two weeks.

**Teaching moment:** Read John 12:24, where Jesus explains His own death: "Unless a kernel of wheat falls to the ground and dies, it remains only a single seed. But if it dies, it produces many seeds." He gave us the illustration Himself, days before it happened.

## 9. Palm Sunday Parade

**Materials:** Real palm fronds if you have them, or green construction paper cut into leaf shapes and taped to paint stirrers. Cloaks, jackets, or towels for the road.

Line the hallway or driveway. One child rides a scooter, a bike, or a stick horse as the donkey. Everyone else waves branches, lays down jackets, and shouts "Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!" Do a lap, swap the rider, do it again.

**Teaching moment:** After the parade, ask why Jesus picked a donkey instead of a war horse. Kings rode horses to war and donkeys in peace -- Zechariah 9:9 predicted this exact entrance about 500 years earlier.

## 10. Footwashing Night

**Materials:** A basin of warm water, a towel, and a quiet room. That is all.

On Thursday evening, read John 13:1-17 and then actually do it. Wash your kids' feet. Then let them wash each other's, and yours. Expect giggling, and let it happen -- the awkwardness is instructive.

**Teaching moment:** The most important person in that room did the job nobody else wanted. Ask afterward: what is the "foot washing" job in our house this week, and who is going to do it without being asked?

## 11. Holy Week Countdown Chain

**Materials:** Strips of purple and white paper, glue or tape, a marker.

Make eight links, one per day from Palm Sunday to Easter. Write the day's scene and reading on each -- Palm Sunday (Matthew 21), the temple (Matthew 21:12-17), teaching in Jerusalem, the Last Supper (Luke 22), the cross (Luke 23), the tomb, the silent Saturday, and Easter. Use purple for the week and one white link for Sunday. Tear off a link each day.

**Teaching moment:** Kids track time physically. Watching the chain shrink toward a single white link builds real anticipation, and the shape of the week sinks in without a lesson. ## 12. Easter Story Stones

**Materials:** Ten smooth flat stones, paint pens or acrylic paint, a small drawstring bag.

Paint one simple symbol per stone: a donkey, a cup, praying hands, a rope, a crown of thorns, a cross, a linen wrap, a big round stone, a sunrise, a cloud. Keep them crude -- kids should paint them, not you.

Dump the bag out, mix them up, and let your child put them in story order and retell the whole thing from memory. Then hand the bag to a younger sibling and let the older one teach it.

**Teaching moment:** Retelling is how a story becomes theirs. If they get stuck on the order, [the Easter story in the Bible](/blog/easter-story-in-the-bible) walks through all ten scenes with the scripture for each, and the videos at [Faithful Kids](https://faithfulkids.app/quiz) cover the same scenes one at a time.

## 13. A Verse in Every Egg

**Materials:** Your normal plastic eggs, small strips of paper, and a pen.

Before you hide the eggs, tuck a verse strip into each one alongside the candy. Whoever finds it reads the verse out loud before eating anything. Twelve to twenty verses is plenty -- pull them from our list of [50 Easter Bible verses](/blog/easter-bible-verses), starting with Matthew 28:6, Luke 24:5-6, John 11:25, and 1 Corinthians 15:55.

**Teaching moment:** You do not have to choose between a candy hunt and a Christian Easter. Adding one step turns a twenty-minute sugar sprint into twenty verses read aloud in your backyard, and no child has ever complained about it.

## 14. Easter Cards for People Who Are Alone

**Materials:** Cardstock, markers, stamps, and a list of names -- a widowed neighbor, a homebound church member, a family member in a care facility.

Have each child make three cards. Write one verse inside each, in their own handwriting. Deliver them in person if you can, on the Saturday before Easter.

**Teaching moment:** Easter is news, and news is meant to be carried. This is the activity that moves the story from something kids receive to something they hand to somebody else. For card-sized verses, 1 Peter 1:3 and 1 Corinthians 15:57 are the two that fit best.

## 15. Sunrise Chalk Mural

**Materials:** Sidewalk chalk and a driveway, or butcher paper taped along a hallway wall.

On Easter morning, draw the empty tomb across the driveway together -- the hill, the open doorway, the stone rolled aside, the sunrise behind it. Write "He Is Risen" big enough for the street to read. Everyone contributes something, including the two-year-old.

**Teaching moment:** The first Christians could not keep this quiet, and Mary Magdalene's first sermon was four words long: "I have seen the Lord!" (John 20:18). Putting it on the driveway where neighbors walk past is the kid-sized version of the same instinct.

## How to Pick the Right Ones for Your Family

If you only do one, do the resurrection eggs. Twelve days, five minutes each, and the whole story arrives in your child's hands one object at a time. If you have older kids who find eggs babyish, use the story stones and let them teach the younger ones instead.

For ages 3-5, stick with the parade, the rolls, the butterfly, and the empty tomb garden -- all visual, none requiring the crucifixion in detail. For 6-9, add the eggs, the story stones, the seed, and the verse hunt. For 10 and up, the overnight cookies, footwashing night, and the card ministry carry more weight, because the lesson is about cost rather than color.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What goes in resurrection eggs?

The traditional twelve items are a donkey or palm leaf, silver coins, bread or a cup, praying hands, a rope, thorns, a cross, a nail, dice, spices or linen, a stone, and an empty twelfth egg. The full list with the scripture for each is at the top of this page. You can substitute freely -- the objects only need to trigger the memory of the scene.

### What age are resurrection eggs for?

Ages 3 to 10 is the sweet spot, with the peak around 5 to 8. Younger toddlers enjoy the opening but will not track the sequence, and kids over 10 usually prefer being the one who runs the activity for younger siblings. Keep egg 8, the nail, short and simple for preschoolers.

### What is a good Easter activity for a Sunday school class?

The palm parade, the stone-rolling challenge, story stones, and a verse-in-every-egg hunt all scale to a class of twenty with almost no prep. Resurrection rolls work if you have kitchen access; if not, prep them at home and bring the baked ones plus the raw ingredients to demonstrate. Story stones give every child something to take home.

### How do I keep Easter focused on Jesus without banning the fun?

You do not have to remove the candy -- you have to attach the story to it. Put a verse in every egg, do the hunt on Saturday and give Sunday morning to the resurrection, or make the empty egg the prize egg. Kids who grow up with both rarely confuse which one is the point, as long as the adults are clear about it.

### When should we start these activities?

Most families start at the beginning of Holy Week, but the twelve-day activities like resurrection eggs and the countdown chain work better if you begin about two weeks out. The seed planting and the empty tomb garden need Good Friday specifically. If you observe the longer season, [Lent for kids explained](/blog/lent-for-kids-explained) covers the six-week run-up.

### What if my child is upset by the crucifixion parts?

Slow down and shorten it. Say less about what was done to Jesus and more about why He chose it, and never end a session on Friday -- read or tell the empty tomb in the same sitting, even if it is just one sentence. If a child stays anxious, switch to the butterfly, the seed, and the garden activities, which teach resurrection through new life rather than through the cross, and revisit the harder scenes in a year.
