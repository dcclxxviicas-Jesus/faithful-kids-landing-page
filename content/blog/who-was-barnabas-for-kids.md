# Who Was Barnabas in the Bible? Story for Kids

Some Bible heroes part seas or topple giants. Barnabas' superpower was different: he believed in people before anyone else did — and the two people he believed in, when nobody else would, turned out to be the apostle Paul and the Gospel-writer Mark. Remove Barnabas from the story and you lose half the New Testament.

His real name was Joseph. The apostles gave him the nickname that stuck: Barnabas, "son of encouragement" (Acts 4:36). You earn a nickname like that.

## The Generous Beginning (Acts 4:32-37)

Barnabas first appears in the young Jerusalem church as an example of open-handedness: a Levite from Cyprus who "sold a field he owned and brought the money and put it at the apostles' feet" (Acts 4:37).

Luke names him right before telling the story of Ananias and Sapphira, who faked the same kind of gift for show. The contrast is the point: Barnabas gave quietly and genuinely. His first recorded act is simply generosity.

**Key lesson for kids:** Encouragement starts with open hands. Before Barnabas ever said an encouraging word in Scripture, he gave — people trust encouragers whose generosity is real.

## The Man Who Vouched for Saul (Acts 9:26-27)

After Saul the persecutor met Jesus on the Damascus road ([Saul Meets Jesus](/blog/saul-meets-jesus-for-kids)), he tried to join the disciples in Jerusalem — and they were all afraid of him. Reasonable! The last they'd heard, Saul was arresting Christians. Church doors stayed shut.

One man took the risk: "Barnabas took him and brought him to the apostles" (Acts 9:27), told Saul's story for him, and staked his own good name on the church's most famous enemy.

Think about what hung on that moment. If nobody vouches for Saul, there may be no missionary journeys, no thirteen letters, no [Paul](/blog/who-was-paul-for-kids) as we know him.

**Key lesson for kids:** Every "new kid" needs one person to say "come sit with us — I'll vouch for you." Barnabas shows how much can hang on that small, slightly risky kindness. Be the one who goes first.

## Partner on the First Journey (Acts 11-14)

When the gospel unexpectedly caught fire among Greeks in Antioch, the Jerusalem church sent Barnabas to investigate. His reaction tells you everything: "he saw what the grace of God had done" and "was glad" (Acts 11:23) — no suspicion, just joy. Luke pauses to give him the Bible's best one-line reference: "He was a good man, full of the Holy Spirit and faith."

Then Barnabas did a very Barnabas thing: he traveled to Tarsus, found Saul — who had been off the main stage for years — and brought him to Antioch to help teach. Together they taught for a year in the church where "the disciples were first called Christians" (Acts 11:26).

From Antioch, the Spirit sent them out on the first missionary journey — Cyprus (Barnabas' home island), then into Asia Minor, planting churches through stonings and triumphs alike (trace their route on our free [map of Paul's missionary journeys](/printables/bible-maps/pauls-missionary-journeys)). In Lystra the astonished crowds even mistook them for gods — and notice which one they called Zeus, the senior god: Barnabas (Acts 14:12).

**Key lesson for kids:** Barnabas spent his influence promoting someone else. He went and got Saul, shared his platform, and gradually let "Barnabas and Saul" become "Paul and Barnabas" — and Scripture never records him minding. Greatness that makes room for others is the rarest kind.

## The Fight Over a Second Chance (Acts 15:36-41)

Here's the honest part. Planning their second journey, Paul and Barnabas hit a wall over one person: John Mark, Barnabas' young cousin, who had quit and gone home partway through the first journey. Barnabas wanted to bring him again. Paul refused to take a quitter.

"They had such a sharp disagreement that they parted company" (Acts 15:39). Barnabas sailed for Cyprus with Mark; Paul chose Silas and went overland ([Paul and Barnabas Split](/blog/paul-and-barnabas-split-for-kids) tells it for kids).

The Bible doesn't referee the argument. But it shows the fruit: years later Paul himself wrote, "Get Mark and bring him with you, because he is helpful to me in my ministry" (2 Timothy 4:11) — and that restored quitter, tradition holds, wrote the Gospel of Mark. Barnabas' second chance worked.

**Key lesson for kids:** Good people can disagree strongly and both keep serving God — the mission doubled instead of dying. And someone who fails once isn't finished: Mark's comeback, purchased by Barnabas' patience, gave the world a Gospel.

## Why Barnabas Matters

- **He's the reason the church got Paul.** One brave introduction in Acts 9, one recruiting trip to Tarsus in Acts 11 — no one else was willing.
- **He's the reason the church kept Mark.** His stubborn second chance restored the young man who became Mark the evangelist.
- **He models encouragement as a calling.** Giving, vouching, rejoicing at others' success, sharing the spotlight — Acts treats these as Spirit-filled work, not personality traits.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does the name Barnabas mean?

Acts 4:36 translates it "son of encouragement." His given name was Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus; the apostles renamed him for what he kept doing. In the Bible, "son of" often describes character — a "son of encouragement" is encouragement personified. It remains one of the best nicknames anyone in Scripture ever earned.

### Who was right in the fight between Paul and Barnabas?

Scripture deliberately doesn't say. Paul had a real point — the work was dangerous, and Mark had already quit once. Barnabas had a real point too — people grow, and Mark deserved another shot. The outcome suggests God used both: two mission teams went out instead of one, Mark was restored (Paul later asked for him by name, 2 Timothy 4:11), and Paul and Barnabas clearly remained respectful — Paul mentions Barnabas warmly in later letters (1 Corinthians 9:6).

### Was Barnabas an apostle?

Not one of the Twelve, but Acts 14:14 does call Barnabas and Paul "apostles" in the broader sense — "sent ones," commissioned by the church at Antioch under the Spirit's direction (Acts 13:2-3). He was also a prophet and teacher (Acts 13:1) and one of the most trusted leaders of the early church, chosen to carry both famine relief (Acts 11:30) and the landmark Jerusalem council letter (Acts 15:22-26).

### What happened to Barnabas after Acts?

Acts last shows him sailing to Cyprus with Mark (Acts 15:39). Paul's letters confirm he kept working as a missionary who, like Paul, supported himself (1 Corinthians 9:6). Early tradition says he continued ministering on Cyprus and died there as a martyr — the island church has honored him as its founder ever since.
