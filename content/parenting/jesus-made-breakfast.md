Of all the things the risen Jesus could have done on that grey morning by the Sea of Galilee, he made breakfast.

The disciples had been fishing all night and caught nothing. They were cold, discouraged, and not at all sure what came next. Then a figure on the shore called out to them, the net filled, and when they dragged it to land they found that someone had already lit a fire. "They saw a fire of burning coals there with fish on it, and some bread" (John 21:9). Then came the most ordinary invitation in the whole of the Gospels: "Come and have breakfast" (John 21:12).

He had conquered the grave. He held all authority in heaven and on earth. And he spent that morning cooking fish for his tired friends, then handing it to them with his own hands (John 21:13).

I think about that scene most mornings when I am standing at my stove, half awake, cracking eggs into a pan while small people in pyjamas drift into the kitchen. It is easy to think of cooking for children as a chore that sits beneath the important work of raising them in faith. [Breakfast on the beach](/blog/breakfast-on-the-beach-for-kids) says otherwise. Feeding people is one of the oldest ways God has of showing them they are loved.

## The Bible is a book full of kitchens

Once you start noticing the food in Scripture, you see it everywhere. Our faith did not grow up in a lecture hall. It grew up around fires and tables, with flour on its hands.

When three visitors arrived at Abraham's tent in the heat of the day, he did not offer them a glass of water and a polite conversation. He ran. He hurried to Sarah and said, "Quick, get three seahs of the finest flour and knead it and bake some bread" (Genesis 18:6). The finest flour. Then curds and milk and a tender calf, prepared and set before his guests while he stood near them under a tree. Abraham's welcome was something you could taste.

[Jacob's lentil stew](/blog/jacob-and-esau-for-kids) is famous for the wrong reasons: Esau came in from the field so hungry that he traded his birthright for a bowl of it (Genesis 25:29-34). Children love this story, partly because they understand that kind of hunger far better than adults do. But notice what the story assumes. A pot of red lentils simmering over a fire smelled so good that a grown man could not walk past it. Good food has power. That is exactly why it is worth making well.

When God described the promised land to his people, he described it as a pantry: "a land with wheat and barley, vines and fig trees, pomegranates, olive oil and honey; a land where bread will not be scarce" (Deuteronomy 8:8-9). And he added an instruction: "When you have eaten and are satisfied, praise the Lord your God for the good land he has given you" (Deuteronomy 8:10). Eat, be satisfied, give thanks. It is the shape of a family meal.

In [the story of Ruth](/blog/ruth-and-boaz-for-kids), a foreign widow gleaning in a stranger's field is invited to the table at mealtime: "Come over here. Have some bread and dip it in the wine vinegar" (Ruth 2:14). Boaz offers her roasted grain, and she eats until she is full and has some left over. Kindness, in the book of Ruth, arrives as lunch.

Even Daniel's courage in Babylon came down to what was on his plate. He asked to be given vegetables and water instead of the king's rich food, and at the end of ten days he and his friends "looked healthier and better nourished" than everyone else (Daniel 1:15). Simple, wholesome food, chosen with care.

> He had conquered the grave. And he spent that morning cooking fish for his tired friends.

## "If your son asks for bread"

Jesus used food to explain the heart of God to ordinary parents. "Which of you, if your son asks for bread, will give him a stone? Or if he asks for a fish, will give him a snake? If you then, though you are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good gifts to those who ask him!" (Matthew 7:9-11).

Read that again with a kitchen in mind. Jesus takes it for granted that a loving parent feeds their child well. That is the picture he reaches for when he wants to describe God's generosity. When we put good food in front of our children, made with care, we are acting out a small parable of the Father who gives good gifts.

That is why I have come to believe that cooking well for our children matters, and not mainly for reasons of nutrition, important as those are. A meal made by someone who loves you says something a packet cannot. It says: you were thought about today. Someone chose this for you, stood at the stove for you, and set it down in front of you because you matter. "Taste and see that the Lord is good," says the psalmist (Psalm 34:8). Children learn about goodness through their senses first. A warm bowl of soup on a cold evening is a lesson they will understand long before they understand a doctrine.

## Good food does not have to mean fancy food

I want to say this clearly, because I know how guilt can creep into a conversation about feeding children. Good food is not complicated food. It is not expensive food, or photographed food, or food that takes all afternoon. Most of the meals in the Bible were astonishingly simple: bread, fish, lentils, barley, honey, fruit. When Jesus fed the five thousand, he started with a boy's lunch of "five small barley loaves and two small fish" (John 6:9). Ordinary food, blessed and shared, was enough for a multitude.

So good food, in my kitchen, means real food, cooked with attention, most of the time. A pot of soup that lasts three days. Eggs and toast. Roasted vegetables and a chicken. A loaf of bread on a Saturday, when there is time. It also means giving myself grace on the nights when dinner comes out of the freezer. The point is not perfection. The point is that, more often than not, the people I love sit down to something I made for them.

## Cooking the Bible with your children

One of the joys of noticing all this food in Scripture is that you can cook it. Children who have stirred a story remember it far better than children who only heard it. A few ideas from our own kitchen:

- **Esau's red lentil stew.** Red lentils, an onion, a carrot, a little cumin, simmered until soft. Read Genesis 25 while it cooks and ask whether anything would make you trade something precious for a bowl of soup. Children always have opinions.
- **Abraham's quick bread.** A simple flatbread of flour, water, salt and oil, pressed by small hands and cooked in a hot pan. Talk about how Abraham hurried to welcome strangers, and who you might welcome to your own table.
- **Breakfast on the beach.** Fish and bread for breakfast, once, as a treat, perhaps outside if the weather allows. Read John 21 and let the children hand out the food the way Jesus did.
- **A promised-land plate.** Set out the foods of Deuteronomy 8: bread, figs, grapes, pomegranate seeds, olive oil for dipping, a little honey. Let the children taste each one and say thank you for each, out loud, the way the passage tells us to.
- **Daniel's vegetables.** A big tray of roasted vegetables, chosen and chopped by the children. Ask why Daniel cared so much about what he ate, and what it means to look after the body God gave us.

None of these need to be elaborate, and none of them need to be done all at once. One a month is plenty. The goal is a kitchen where the Bible is not only read but smelled and tasted and shared.

## Known in the breaking of bread

There is one more meal I want to mention, because it is the one I think of whenever my family finally sits down together at the end of a long day. Two disciples walked to Emmaus with a stranger who explained the Scriptures to them, and they still did not recognise him. Then he sat down to eat with them. "He took bread, gave thanks, broke it and began to give it to them. Then their eyes were opened and they recognized him" (Luke 24:30-31). They knew him [at the table](/blog/the-road-to-emmaus-for-kids).

I do not think that is an accident. Something happens at a table that does not happen anywhere else. We slow down. We look at each other. We give thanks. And sometimes, in the middle of passing the bread, we recognise the presence of the One who gave it.

So cook for your children. Not perfectly, and not every night, but often, and with love. Light the stove the way Jesus lit that fire on the beach. Make the stew, bake the bread, roast the vegetables. Then call them in, the way he called his friends: come and have breakfast. It is one of the most faithful things a parent can do. [Every home is teaching something](/parenting/every-home-is-teaching-something), and the kitchen teaches it three times a day.
