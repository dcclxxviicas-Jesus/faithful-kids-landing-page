I wrote earlier this year, in The Christian Post, about the season I took my kids' screens away and why it did not work. I am not going to retell that piece here. I want to say the thing I have become more sure of since: the fight most Christian parents think they are in is the wrong fight.

We talk about screen time as if the unit of harm were the minute. Thirty minutes good, three hours bad. Set a timer, hold the line, feel guilty when it slips. That framing made sense when a screen was a television with four channels and a sign-off at midnight. It makes no sense now, because the thing on the other side of the glass is no longer a program. It is a feed, and a feed is designed by very capable people to make sure there is never a natural place to stop.

## The screen is not the enemy. The feed is.

Think about what actually happens when a child watches something that was made to be watched. A story starts, something happens, it ends. There is a last page, a credits roll, a moment where the child looks up and is back in the room. That moment is where parenting happens. It is where you ask what they thought, where they tell you the part that scared them, where you find out they understood the opposite of what you expected.

A feed removes that moment on purpose. Autoplay, infinite scroll, the next thumbnail already loading: every one of those is an engineering decision whose goal is that the child never looks up. The harm is not that the child saw too many minutes. It is that the child was never handed back.

So when I took the screens away, I removed the feed, which was good, but I also removed every story, every song, every documentary about volcanoes, and every chance to sit next to my kid and watch something worth talking about. Then I gave the screens back, because that is what happens in a real house, and the feed came back with them. I had fought the glass and lost to the algorithm.

## What I fight now

I fight for endings. That sounds small. It is the whole thing.

A show that ends is a show you can talk about. A video that ends with a question is a video that insists on the conversation. A device that has nothing autoplaying after the thing you chose is a device your child can put down without a struggle, because nothing is pulling.

In practice that means a few rules in our house that have held better than any timer:

- **No feeds for children.** Not YouTube, not YouTube Kids, not a short-form app of any kind. A child can watch something we chose, from a library we trust, that ends.
- **Pick, then watch.** The choosing happens before the device is on. "What are we watching tonight?" is a question for the dinner table, not for the thumbnail grid.
- **Something has to come after.** A question, a drawing, a verse we say back, a walk. The thing after the screen is what turns watching into learning, and it is also what makes the ending feel like an ending instead of a loss.
- **We watch some of it with them.** Not all. Some. Enough that the child knows the screen is not a place parents never go.

None of these needs a timer, and none of them produces the fight that timers produce, because none of them asks a child to stop in the middle of something that was built to have no middle.

> The question is not how many minutes. The question is whether, at the end of them, your child is handed back to you.

## Where Scripture actually leans on this

I am wary of proof-texting parenting decisions, so let me be modest. The Bible does not have a view on tablets. It has a strong view on what fills a child's attention and who is responsible for it. "These commandments that I give you today are to be on your hearts. Impress them on your children. Talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up" (Deuteronomy 6:6-7). That is a description of a life with a lot of talking in it, at the ordinary moments. A feed is a machine for eliminating ordinary moments. That is the conflict, and it is a real one, and it is not about minutes.

Paul's "whatever is true, whatever is noble, whatever is right, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely" (Philippians 4:8) is usually quoted at the content, and fairly. But notice that it is addressed to the one doing the thinking. It assumes a person who chooses what to dwell on. A child in a feed is not choosing. The choosing has been outsourced to a system whose idea of "lovely" is whatever keeps the thumb moving.

## The honest part

I make a screen for children now. My company builds Bible video lessons, and children watch them on the same tablets I used to confiscate. I would be lying if I said that was not awkward to write in an essay like this.

Here is how I square it, and you can judge whether it holds. Every lesson we make ends. It ends with a question the child has to answer, and then one they talk about with a parent. There is no feed, nothing autoplays, and there are no ads, because an ad is just a tiny feed with a budget. I built it that way because of the season I describe in the Christian Post piece, not in spite of it. If I had found something else that worked that way, I would have used it.

But I am not here to tell you to use what I make. I am here to tell you that if you are measuring your parenting by minutes, you are measuring the wrong thing, and the people who built the feed would like you to keep doing that. Measure endings. Count the number of times this week your child finished something, looked up, and told you about it. That number is the one that matters, and it is the one you can actually move.
