Ask a child what happened in the story of Jonah and you will get an answer: a big fish, a city, a man who ran away. Ask the same child whether God was unfair to the plant and you will get a silence, then a frown, then something you did not expect. The first question checks whether they were listening. The second one is where the story gets in.

Most of what we call Bible teaching for children is built around the first kind of question. That is not a criticism. Comprehension matters, and a child who cannot tell you who built the ark is not ready to wonder why. But if comprehension is where we stop, we have taught a child that the Bible is a set of facts to be retrieved rather than a conversation to be entered. And the conversation is the point.

## Two kinds of questions

A quiz question has an answer the asker already knows. "How many days was Jonah in the fish?" The child's job is to produce it. There is a right and a wrong, and the exchange ends when the right one appears.

A wonder question does not have an answer the asker already knows, or at least not one the asker is sure of. "Why do you think Jonah was angry when the city was saved?" Nobody at the table owns that one. The child's job is not to retrieve but to think, and the exchange does not end when an answer appears. It opens.

Children know the difference instantly. They can tell when they are being tested and when they are being consulted, and they bring a completely different part of themselves to each. The tested child performs. The consulted child reveals.

## Why the unanswerable question forms a child

Three things happen when you ask a question you cannot answer.

**The child learns that the Bible is big enough to hold their questions.** A great deal of faith is lost in the teenage years not because teenagers stop believing but because they discover questions and conclude, wrongly, that the adults around them never had any. A child who has watched a parent say "I honestly do not know, let's think about it" over an open Bible at seven has been inoculated against that discovery at fourteen.

**The child meets the story as a story about people.** Jonah's anger, David's fear, Peter's shame, Martha's irritation: these are the hinges of the narratives, and they are exactly the parts a comprehension quiz skips, because feelings do not have verifiable answers. Ask about them and the characters stop being flannelgraph and start being human. That is when a child begins to recognise themselves.

**You find out what your child actually thinks.** This is the practical gift. Children hold theology you would never guess at. They believe God is disappointed in them for things they have never told you. They think Judas had no choice. They wonder whether the animals on the ark were scared. You will not learn any of that from a quiz. You will learn all of it from one good question and a long enough silence afterward.

> A child who has heard a parent say "I do not know" over an open Bible at seven is ready for the hard questions at fourteen.

## Jesus asked questions

It is worth noticing that the only glimpse Scripture gives us of Jesus as a child shows him asking questions. Luke records the twelve-year-old in the temple, "sitting among the teachers, listening to them and asking them questions," and everyone who heard him "was amazed at his understanding and his answers" (Luke 2:46-47). The understanding and the questions arrive together. He did not ask because he lacked answers. He asked because that is how understanding moves between people.

Deuteronomy assumes the same thing from the other direction. "In the future, when your son asks you, 'What is the meaning of the stipulations, decrees and laws the Lord our God has commanded you?' tell him" (Deuteronomy 6:20-21). The pattern of faith passed down is not a lecture; it is a child's question and a parent's story. The text does not say "when you test your son." It says when he asks.

## Three questions for tonight

You do not need a curriculum for this. You need the story, a little courage, and a willingness to sit in the pause. After any Bible story, try one of these:

1. **"Who in this story would you not want to be, and why?"** Children pick the obvious villain first. Let them. Then ask about the second-least-enviable person, the one who was afraid or who stayed quiet, and watch the conversation turn.
2. **"What do you think God was feeling here?"** Not what God did. What God felt. This question has startled more adults than children, and it is the fastest road from facts to relationship.
3. **"Is there anything in this story that seems unfair?"** Then do not rush to fix it. The unfairness a child spots is often the exact place the story is doing its work, and your job is to wonder alongside, not to close the case.

If the honest answer to any of these is "I am not sure," say so. You are not failing the lesson. You are modelling the thing the lesson is for.

## What to do with the quiz

Keep it. A short check that the story went in is useful, and children like getting things right. But put it in its place: a quiz is the doorway, not the room. The room is the question you cannot answer, asked by someone the child trusts, with enough quiet afterwards for the child to walk in.
