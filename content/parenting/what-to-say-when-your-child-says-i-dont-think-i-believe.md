It almost never happens where you would expect. Not in a church pew, not during family devotions, not after a sermon on the hard parts of Scripture. It happens on a Tuesday, in the car, with the radio low and the wipers going, when your twelve-year-old looks out the window and says, very quietly, "I don't think I believe any of it."

Your hands tighten on the wheel. Your mind runs ahead to every fear you have ever had about this child. And in the half second before you speak, you have a choice that will shape the next ten years of conversations: you can treat the sentence as an emergency, or you can treat it as a door.

I want to make the case for the door.

## First, hear what was actually said

A child who says "I don't think I believe" has done something brave. They have told you the truth about the inside of their head, knowing it might upset you. That is not rebellion. That is trust. The very fact that they said it to you, and not only to a friend or a search bar, means the relationship is working.

So the first job is not to answer. It is to receive. Something as simple as "Thank you for telling me that" buys you time, lowers the temperature, and tells your child that honesty is safe in this family. If you get nothing else right in that moment, get that.

The second job is to find out what the sentence means, because it means different things at different ages and on different days. Sometimes it means "I learned something at school that seems to contradict what we say at church." Sometimes it means "I prayed for something and nothing happened." Sometimes it means "My friend says it is all made up and I didn't know what to say back." And sometimes it means "I am tired, and church is boring, and I want to see what you do."

You cannot answer any of those until you know which one you are hearing. So ask. "What got you thinking about that?" "When did you start feeling that way?" "Is there a part that seems hardest to believe?" Then listen for longer than is comfortable.

## Scripture is full of people who said this out loud

One of the most freeing things you can tell a doubting child is that they are in good company, and that the company is in the Bible.

A father brings his son to Jesus and begs for help. Jesus says everything is possible for one who believes, and the father answers with one of the most honest prayers ever recorded: "I do believe; help me overcome my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24). Jesus does not rebuke him for the second half of the sentence. He heals the boy.

[Thomas](/blog/doubting-thomas-for-kids) hears from his friends that Jesus is alive and refuses to accept it without seeing for himself (John 20:24-29). We have given him a nickname that sounds like an insult, but notice what Jesus does a week later. He comes back, finds Thomas, and offers him exactly the evidence he asked for. Doubt did not get Thomas thrown out of the room. It got him a personal visit.

The writer of Psalm 73 admits that he nearly lost his footing altogether, that faith looked pointless when he watched the world. The psalm is in the hymnbook of Israel. God's people sang their doubts.

When a child hears these stories from you, at the very moment they are doubting, the message is unmistakable: questions are not the opposite of faith. They are one of the places faith gets worked out.

> Doubt did not get Thomas thrown out of the room. It got him a personal visit.

## What not to say

Most of the damage in these conversations comes from fear, and fear has a few favourite lines. It is worth knowing them in advance so you can catch yourself.

- **"You don't really mean that."** They do. Telling a child they cannot be feeling what they just said they feel teaches them to stop saying it.
- **"How could you say that after everything?"** This turns their honesty into a debt they owe you. The next time, they will pay it with silence.
- **The thirty-minute apologetics lecture.** Arguments have their place, and some children will want them. But a flood of answers to a question nobody has finished asking sounds like you are trying to win, not trying to understand.
- **"We'll talk about it later," and then never doing so.** Postponing is fine. Forgetting is not. If you need a day to think, say so, and then come back to it yourself.

## What helps

None of this is a script, and every child is different. But a few things help almost every time.

- **Tell the truth about your own questions.** If you have ever wondered, say so. "There were years when I wasn't sure either" may be the most reassuring sentence your child hears all year, because it tells them that doubt is survivable and that the person they trust most came through it. Peter had that kind of moment. When many people walked away from Jesus, Peter did not say he had every answer. He said, "Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life" (John 6:68).
- **Take the question seriously enough to look into it together.** If the doubt is about science, or suffering, or why there are so many religions, find something good to read on it and read it side by side. Our job, Peter writes, is to be ready to give a reason for our hope "with gentleness and respect" (1 Peter 3:15). That applies at the kitchen table too. It is the same instinct behind [the best Bible question being the one you cannot answer](/parenting/the-best-bible-question-is-the-one-you-cannot-answer): the not-knowing, faced together, builds more trust than a quick answer ever does.
- **Keep the rhythms, loosen the grip.** Keep going to church as a family, keep praying at dinner, keep the bedtime Bible story if it is still part of your nights. Do not turn any of them into a test. A child who is doubting needs the family's faith to stay steady and warm around them, not to tighten.
- **Pray for them, and tell them you are praying.** Not as a threat. As a promise. "I'm going to keep praying for you, because I love you and I want you to find what's true" is a sentence a child can carry.
- **Be merciful.** Jude's instruction to the early church was short and plain: "Be merciful to those who doubt" (Jude 1:22). If that is how the church is meant to treat doubters, it is certainly how a parent is.

## The long view

Here is what I most want a frightened parent to hear. A child's faith is rarely decided in one conversation, and almost never in the first one. What is decided in the first one is whether there will be a second.

Jesus told a story about a father whose son walked away entirely, and the detail that has comforted parents for two thousand years is that the father was watching the road (Luke 15:20). He saw [the prodigal son](/blog/the-prodigal-son-for-kids) "while he was still a long way off" and ran. Most children who say "I don't think I believe" are nowhere near that far country. They are sitting in the passenger seat, testing whether it is safe to think out loud.

Make it safe. Ask before you answer. Tell them about Thomas, and about the father who said "help my unbelief." Keep the porch light on. A faith that has been allowed to ask its hardest questions at home is far more likely to still be standing at thirty than one that was never allowed to ask them at all.
