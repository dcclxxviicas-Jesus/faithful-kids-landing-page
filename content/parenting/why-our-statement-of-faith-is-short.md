For most of this year Faithful Kids did not have a statement of faith. It had Bible stories, retold from the text with the reference on screen, and I thought the text was the statement. Then, in one week in September, four different homeschool-group leaders wrote to ask for one before they would recommend us to their families. The message underneath was the same each time: we like what you make, but we need to know who you are before we put you in front of our kids.

That is a completely fair ask. It is also harder to answer well than it looks, and the answering taught me something about what a Bible tool for children owes the families who use it.

## Who the statement is for

The families who use Faithful Kids are Catholic, Evangelical, Orthodox, Baptist, Reformed, Pentecostal and non-denominational. Not a theoretical mix: those are the churches and groups that have written to us. A children's Bible video that quotes Genesis with the reference on screen does not belong to any one of those traditions. A statement of faith, if I was careless, easily could.

So the instruction I gave myself, and the one I have asked everyone who touches that page to keep, was this: say what is most generalizable, and say nothing that goes against the beliefs of the Christian families we serve. Not "say as little as possible." Say the real thing, and stop at the line where Christians who share the Gospel begin to divide.

## The two words I would not add

The page says, "The Bible is the inspired, true Word of God." More than one reader has suggested it should continue, "and our final authority." I understand why. It sounds firmer. It is also a specific position: Scripture as the sole final authority is a Protestant conviction, and Catholic and Orthodox Christians, who affirm with their whole hearts that the Bible is inspired and true, hold it alongside the teaching of the Church. Adding four words would turn a sentence every Christian family can hand to their children into a sentence that quietly tells a third of them this is not for you.

The page also says, "Salvation is found in him, by grace through faith." That is Ephesians 2:8, nearly word for word, and it is universal. One more word, "alone," makes it a Reformation slogan. I believe the Reformation debates matter. I also believe a seven-year-old watching the story of Zacchaeus does not need her Bible app to have taken a side in them, and her parents did not ask it to.

> A statement of faith for children should be something a child's own church would not have to correct.

## What it costs

I want to be honest about the trade. A short, generalizable statement will strike some readers as thin, or as hedging. Some ministries will want to see the distinctives of their tradition named before they trust us, and we will not be able to give them that. A few have told me so, politely, and declined. I respect it. The alternative was to choose a tradition and lose everyone else, or worse, to write a statement vague enough to mean nothing, which is a different kind of dishonesty.

What I will not do is pretend the page says more than it does. The app does not have a Catholic track and a Protestant track. The lessons are the same for everyone, because they are the stories of the Bible with the verses on screen, and we leave the catechesis to your church, where it belongs. When a question comes in about baptism, or the end times, or how to read Genesis 1, my answer is the same: ask your pastor. That is not evasion. It is the honest limit of what a video app should be doing in a family's life.

## Why this matters beyond our page

I think the same principle applies to every parent choosing resources for their kids, which is why I am writing about it here rather than leaving it as a footer link.

Christian parents are offered a great deal of material that smuggles distinctives into content that presents itself as simply biblical. Some of it is excellent. But a child cannot tell the difference between "the Bible says" and "our tradition's reading of what the Bible says," and a resource that blurs that line is making a decision that belongs to you and your church.

So when you look at a curriculum, a show, an app, a Sunday school pack, the question I would ask is not only "do I agree with this?" It is "does this know the difference between the Gospel and my tradition's commentary on it, and does it respect that the second part is my job?" A resource that cannot answer that clearly is asking for more trust than it has earned.

Our statement of faith is short because that question has a short answer. Everything else is between your family and your church, and we would like to keep it that way.
