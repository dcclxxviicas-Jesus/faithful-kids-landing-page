When it was over, the world went quiet.

The crowds drifted away. The soldiers pulled out the nails and took my body down from the cross. My mother held me one last time. The sky was still heavy, like the whole earth was holding its breath.

Then a man stepped forward — someone most people didn't expect.

His name was Joseph, from a town called Arimathea. He was rich. He was powerful. He was a member of the Jewish council — the same council that had just condemned me. But Joseph had never agreed with what they did. He had been my follower in secret, too afraid to say it out loud. Until now.

Something about watching me die changed him. Fear lost its grip. Joseph went straight to Pontius Pilate and asked for my body. That took courage. He was publicly identifying himself with a man the government had just executed. His reputation, his position, his safety — he risked all of it.

And he wasn't alone.

Nicodemus came too. You might remember him — he was the Pharisee who had come to me at night, in secret, back in John chapter three. He was the one I told, "You must be born again." That conversation had been haunting him for years. And now, finally, he stopped hiding.

Together, Joseph and Nicodemus wrapped my body in clean linen cloth with seventy-five pounds of burial spices — myrrh and aloes. They carried me to a brand-new tomb carved out of rock, a tomb no one had ever used. They laid me inside and rolled a massive stone over the entrance.

The Pharisees weren't done, though. They went to Pilate the next day and said, "That man claimed He would rise after three days. Post guards at the tomb so His disciples can't steal the body and fake a resurrection." Pilate agreed. Soldiers were stationed at the tomb. An official seal was placed on the stone.

They thought they were trapping me in. They were really just setting the stage for what was coming next.

Friday was the darkest day. Everything looked lost. Every hope shattered. The disciples were scattered, hiding behind locked doors, weeping, terrified.

Then came Saturday. Silent. Empty. Nothing. The longest day any of them had ever lived through.

But here's what nobody knew — not the disciples, not the Pharisees, not the guards, not Pilate:

Sunday was coming.

And nobody saw it coming.

If you're living in a Saturday right now — stuck between the worst day and the best day, between the pain and the promise — I need you to hold on. The story isn't over. It feels like the end. It's not. Sunday is coming.

And when it comes, it changes everything.
